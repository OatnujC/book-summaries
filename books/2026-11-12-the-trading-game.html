Gary Stevenson · 2024 · summarised 12 November 2026

The Trading Game: Gary Stevenson’s confession that never quite confesses

Gary Stevenson’s The Trading Game: A Confession, published by Allen Lane and Penguin in 2024, is a memoir of a short, violently lucrative career on Citibank’s foreign-exchange desk, written by a man who has since become a YouTube economist campaigning for higher taxes on the rich. Joris Luyendijk, in the Guardian, is careful about the genre. This is not Cityboy and it is not The Wolf of Wall Street. Stevenson is clever, and he has a conscience he is willing to take onto the internet. In 2021 he was one of thirty millionaires who signed a letter asking Rishi Sunak to tax wealth more heavily. The book itself is angrier and less resolved than that public second act. It matters because it is one of the few accounts of the post-2008 trading floor written by someone who grew up looking at Canary Wharf from the wrong side of the glass, and because the economic claim he took out of that floor, that inequality can stall a recovery, is now a political argument, not only a trade.

What happened

The early chapters are east London: violence, shortage, and the towers in sight. Stevenson is good at maths, gets into the London School of Economics, and discovers, in his words, that “a lot of rich people expect poor people to be stupid.” He uses that expectation to win a card game the bank uses as a test, and the prize is an internship and then a job. His first bonus, early in 2009, a few months after Lehman Brothers collapsed, is £13,000, more than half what his father earns in a year at the Post Office. Colleagues tell him to buy his dad a Sky Sports subscription. On Saturdays, instead of watching Leyton Orient with his father and a boy from the street, Stevenson lifts weights at Fitness First in Ilford while his dad watches the television he has just been given. The next bonus is almost £400,000. After that, Luyendijk writes, the boy is hooked. Being the most profitable trader in the bank, and in Stevenson’s telling the most profitable in the world, becomes the point of the life.

The trade that made the money was a bet against the recovery everyone else expected. In 2011 rivals thought ultra-low rates would give way to growth and higher rates. Stevenson thought the opposite. The rich were not going to spend their swollen assets back into the shops. They would buy more assets. Everyone else would pay rent, in one form or another, and demand would stay weak. Rates would stay low. He was right about the rates. Simon Nixon, in the Literary Review, thinks he was right for the wrong reason: what actually stopped growth in 2011 was the eurozone debt crisis, not a completed theory of inequality. A later write-up of the book’s economics puts Stevenson’s own nutshell in his voice. “It was us. It was us, wasn’t it? We were the balance.” Inequality would “grow and grow… until it killed the economy that contained it. It wasn’t temporary, it was terminal.” The wealthy have a lower urge to consume. They buy assets, prices rise, ordinary incomes fall, people sell what they have to the same wealthy buyers, and then rent it back. That loop is the doctrine he still teaches.

The bank then sends him to Tokyo while he is plainly unwell. His girlfriend, called Wizard in the book, follows only after she has her own flat and a teaching job. She is not after the money. She asks, “Why do you fight them, Gary? You’ve got enough. Why don’t you just leave?” Stevenson writes: “She was wrong there. It’s never enough. And I’d never leave. Not if it meant letting them win.” He leaves her instead. Earlier, his best friend asks, “What the fuck is it for, Gary?” There is nothing to say, so Stevenson walks out, and they do not see each other for years. The last stretch of the book is not a conversion. It is a fight to make Citigroup pay the deferred bonus, a sum Nixon puts at the centre of the drama, including a $1.26 million figure Stevenson believed he was owed. The bank caves. Back in London he writes, “I don’t care if I win any more, but I should really stop playing alone.”

What the book is trying to be

Luyendijk knows this world from his own reporting. Traders describe the job as a life that eats the person: sleep debt, no security, friends and family receding with every bonus. Banks are not designed as cults, and they behave like them. He thinks it would be harsh to blame Stevenson for the blinkers. Before the bank he was fluffing pillows in a sofa shop for £40 a day, and he had watched friends throw themselves away on drugs. A confession, though, owes the reader a moment of self-knowledge. What did he think the money was for, once it had cost him his health and he did not even want the things it buys? That moment, Luyendijk says, never comes. Four hundred pages are spent lashing parents, the school that expelled him, LSE students, brokers, and managers who delayed the bonus. The book is “well written and often darkly funny,” and it makes a convincing case that high finance is still toxic. It “falls short as a confession,” with no repentance and no amends. Nixon is harsher. The subtitle promises a confession, and Stevenson confesses little except his own brilliance. The financial crisis, the largest in seventy-five years, “barely features,” except as the weather that made the trades easy. The last hundred pages are about getting paid by a bank that taxpayers had already rescued. The outrage a reader might feel about that never seems to cross the narrator’s mind.

What critics and readers push back on

The economic claim has a life outside the memoir, and it is the part worth arguing with. Stevenson’s picture of inequality as a machine that pushes asset prices up and interest rates down can be put in a model, and some of those models do not kill it. What they do not do, critics note, is license the policy leap. Interest rates have been raised since without a wealth tax, and asset prices have cooled against wages. A thesis that says inequality can move rates is not yet a proof that only a wealth tax can move them back. Nixon’s historical point stands beside that: a trader can be directionally right while misnaming the cause, and then build a politics on the misnaming. Former colleagues, according to one detailed review of the row, have disputed the “best in the world” claim and the idea that he was a lone contrarian. That review’s useful distinction is that you do not need the superlative. If he made a large sum in 2011 from a rates view, the view is worth hearing, and the embroidery is a separate question. The risk is that the embroidery becomes the brand. A campaign against inequality that depends on the campaigner having been the smartest man in the room inherits the room’s manners.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, the early bonus chapters are the moral fact of the book. Thirteen thousand pounds, then four hundred thousand, and a Saturday ritual replaced by a gym, is a better picture of what money does to a family than a chart of the Gini coefficient. Second, “it’s never enough” is not only a trader’s vice. It is the sentence that makes the later politics intelligible, and also the sentence that indicts it. Third, a correct bet is not a correct theory. Rates stayed low. The eurozone crisis and a distributional story are not the same explanation. Fourth, the confession stops at the payout. If you want remorse, Luyendijk is right that it is not here. Fifth, the public Stevenson, the one asking for taxes and warning teenagers off easy crypto, is a different document from the narrator who would not let the bank win. Read both, and do not pretend they are the same man on the same page.

Read the book if you want the texture of a London trading floor after 2008 from someone who was not born to it, and if you can stand a voice that is funny, repetitive and rarely kind. It is a poor guide to monetary policy on its own, and a poor model of how to leave a gilded job. It is a sharp account of how a job becomes a game, and how a game can be won in a way that makes the prize feel like a defeat you are still determined to collect.
