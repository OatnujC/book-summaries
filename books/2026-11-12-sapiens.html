Yuval Noah Harari · 2011 · summarised 12 November 2026

Sapiens: Yuval Noah Harari’s brief history, and the revolutions that do all the work

Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind appeared in Hebrew in 2011 and in English in 2014, and it is the book the later Harari volumes keep assuming you have read. The opening, as a later academic essay quotes the English edition, starts from the Big Bang and arrives quickly at a claim: three revolutions shaped history. “The Cognitive Revolution kick-started history about 70,000 years ago, the Agricultural Revolution sped it up about 12,000 years ago and the Scientific Revolution, which got under way only 500 years ago, may well end history and start something completely different.” A Guardian review by Galen Strawson fills in the cascade Harari then runs. The scientific revolution triggers an industrial one about 250 years ago, an information revolution about 50 years ago, and a biotechnological revolution that, in Harari’s suspicion, replaces us with engineered, “amortal” cyborgs who might not die. The book is a story of how an insignificant animal came to dominate the planet by believing things that are not in front of it. It is also, critics say, a story told at a speed that turns a debate into a fact.

The core argument

The cognitive revolution is the hinge. Another Guardian review, a week later, summarises it as a change in the inner wiring: a new kind of language that could not only pass on information but invent worlds. Flexible cooperation in large numbers follows from shared myths. Neanderthals can be outcompeted, dangerous animals wiped out, crops planted, and pyramids raised, the review says, in the mind as much as in the world. Laws, nations, money and gods sit in that category. They are not lies in the ordinary sense, because millions of people acting as if they are real produces real institutions. They are not facts of physics either. Harari’s distinctive move is to put a limited company and a deity in the same box, as tools that let strangers work together. Once that box exists, the rest of the book is a tour of what was put in it.

Agriculture, on his telling, speeds history and does not simply bless it. More food supports more people, and more people can be organised, taxed and diseased. A life of foraging is not romanticised into a paradise, but the revolution is not sold as an obvious improvement in the life of the person who made it. The scientific revolution is different in kind again. It combines a willingness to admit ignorance with a new power over the physical world, and then with empires that carry that power across oceans. The later chapters, which Homo Deus will expand, suggest that the same power turned inward, onto the body and the mind, may end the species that wields it. “Completely different” is the phrase the opening spends the whole book earning, and an academic reader of the project thinks the phrase is the point: the history is an approach to a technological transformation of the human, whether or not Harari uses the word transhumanism.

Myths, wheat, and the laboratory

The pleasure of the book, which both Guardian reviews register, is the swagger. A huge subject is crossed in a voice that will risk a joke and a judgement on the same page. Strawson thinks the best of it really does illuminate, and that the method is a swashbuckle through matters that are vast and intricate. The risk is the same method. A date of 70,000 years, a mutation, a myth: each is doing the work of a literature. Andrew Orlowski, writing in The Critic, notes that the first edition spoke of a linguistic revolution that began 70,000 years ago “and nobody knows why,” and that by the 2014 English edition this had become a cognitive revolution. Language and cognition are related. They are not the same claim. Noam Chomsky, in that account, has called the resulting story fanciful. You can accept the broad point, that large-scale cooperation depends on shared fictions, without accepting that a single genetic moment switched it on.

The agricultural chapters are where readers either convert or get off. If the revolution increased the number of people while making many individual lives harder, then progress is not a straight gift. If the evidence for that hardship is a composite of skeletons, working hours and epidemics, a popular book owes the reader a sense of how contested the composite is. Harari’s style prefers the clean reversal. The scientific chapters have the opposite temptation: to make European empire, the laboratory and the rise in living standards into one plot, so that a moral argument about conquest and a factual argument about vaccines arrive as the same paragraph. The later books inherit both temptations. Sapiens is where they are introduced as a method.

What critics and readers push back on

Strawson’s review is the friendly version of the objection: brilliance of narrative, a dark destination, and a habit of moving so fast that a specialist’s caveat never gets a seat. The academic essay that quotes the opening goes further. Strong statements about the whole of history are not, it says, the sort of claim a measurement could settle, and the book’s attack on religion sits inside an Enlightenment confidence that a scientific story can replace what it dismantles. Orlowski’s point about the missing citations is practical. A reader who wants to check the 70,000 years, or the welfare of the first farmers, is often left with a paragraph instead of a trail. That is a choice about audience. It is also why the book can be loved and assigned and still be a poor authority to quote for a particular fact.

None of this makes the central tool useless. Shared myths as social technology is an idea you can take into a central bank or a parish and still see something. The overreach is the claim that the idea, plus three dated revolutions, is a history of the species rather than a very good essay about it. Harari writes essays at the length of books. The length persuades. The genre is still the essay.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, strangers cooperate at scale by treating fictions as real, and the results, money, law, nation, are not less powerful for being invented. Second, a change that increases the number of people can still be a loss for the person living it. Ask who ate, not only how many. Third, modern science is tied to an admission of ignorance, and also to empires and industries that used the knowledge. Do not take only half of that. Fourth, a date with a round number of millennia is a marker in a story, not a laboratory result you can repeat. Fifth, the sequel about becoming gods is already seeded here. If you dislike the destination, notice that the journey was built to reach it.

Read it once, quickly, for the frame, and then do not cite it against a historian of any one of its centuries. It is the most enjoyable of Harari’s books and the one that made the others possible. The sentence to keep is the one about myths that let us work with people we will never meet. The sentence to hold lightly is any sentence that begins with a date and the word revolution, as if the species turned on a hinge we have already found.
