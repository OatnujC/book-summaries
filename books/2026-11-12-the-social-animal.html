David Brooks · 2011 · summarised 12 November 2026

The Social Animal: David Brooks’s parable of the unconscious mind

David Brooks’s The Social Animal: The Hidden Sources of Love, Character, and Achievement, published by Random House in 2011, is a strange object: a New York Times columnist’s tour of psychology, neuroscience and moral philosophy, poured into the lives of two people who do not exist. Harold and Erica are born, schooled, married, promoted and aged so that Brooks can hang a finding on each scene. The subtitle promises hidden sources. An NPR review by Susan Jane Gilman says the promise is slightly false. The book does not reveal secret formulas. It is “a giant parable about the power of our unconscious,” and a suggestion that we might live better if we understood how we actually think. That suggestion was already in the air in 2011, after a decade of popular behavioural science. Brooks’s contribution was to put it in a story, and then to let the story argue with the meritocratic script he had spent years describing: degrees, corner offices, the right mix of antique and modern in the country house.

The core argument

There are two levels of mind, he says, and the unconscious one does more of the deciding. The philosopher Thomas Nagel, reviewing the book in the New York Times, immediately marks a confusion in the words. Brooks lists the unconscious as “emotions, intuitions, biases, longings, genetic predispositions, character traits and social norms,” and later adds sensations, perceptions, drives and needs. Most of those are conscious in the ordinary sense. You feel them. What Brooks means is that they are not under direct control. You choose from a menu. You do not choose what you like. Nagel thinks this is obvious without a laboratory. The scientific news is not that automatic processes exist. It is which ones, in which settings, push a particular choice. Brooks is less interested in that precision than in the moral reversal. In Slate, Michael Agger quotes the line that carries the book: “The unconscious parts of the mind are not primitive vestiges that need to be conquered in order to make wise decisions. They are not dark caverns of repressed sexual urges. Instead, the unconscious parts of the mind are most of the mind—where most of the decisions and many of the most impressive acts of thinking take place. These submerged processes are the seedbeds of accomplishment.”

Will Wilkinson, in Forbes, compresses the revolutionary picture Brooks thinks the research establishes: emotion over pure reason, social connection over individual choice, character over IQ, and several selves rather than one. Achievement, on this account, is not a plan executed by a calculating agent. It is a character, built in relationships, that meets a situation with a trained gut. Love is not a contract between two rational shoppers. Politics that treats voters as calculators of interest will keep being surprised. The Hamiltonian ideal, Greek thumos, IQ tests, moral reasoning and behavioural economics all get a scene. Gilman hears Malcolm Gladwell in the achievement chapters. That is fair. The pleasure of the book, when it works, is the same pleasure: a study you had not read, attached to a life you can picture.

Harold, Erica, and the research they carry

Harold and Erica are born into the professional class the book knows best. Their childhoods are used for attachment and early brain development, their schooling for the limits of IQ and the power of culture and peers, their courtship for the unconscious dance of status and attraction, their jobs for the kind of practical intelligence that does not show up as a test score. Brooks pauses the plot to explain a finding, then returns to the couple so the finding has a face. PZ Myers, writing in Salon, calls the result a chimera, “an unholy grafting” of a novel and a pile of materialist explanations for why these two turn out happy. Every chapter, he says, whipsaws between an exemplary event, birth, popularity, career, a European holiday, and a digression into the supposed neural reward for the choice that made them fulfilled. Neither half stands alone.

Gilman, who is kinder, notices what the narrative refuses to depict. For a book about the emotional inner life, there is little emotion of the daily kind. Harold and Erica do not live with the compulsions and anxieties that make most people exhausting to themselves. Children, infidelity and ageing are “glossed over in a few pages.” Their problems are concrete, and they solve them promptly, with street smarts and a study. Agger’s irony is sharper. Brooks has spent the book telling us how puny the rational mind is, and then he will not trust the unconscious on the page. “The writing is not suggestive enough—too much is explained, spelled out, referenced, and enumerated.” Where is the story, he asks, the villains, the moment that is felt rather than footnoted? The characters illustrate the thesis. They rarely threaten it. A parable that cannot surprise its author is a lecture with names.

What critics and readers push back on

Nagel’s terminological point is the cleanest, and it matters because the word “unconscious” is doing too much work. If it means “not chosen,” then hunger and a social norm and a visual illusion are one faculty, which they are not. Wilkinson’s point is about synthesis. Brooks touches an astonishing number of researchers and rarely stays long enough to draw out what a body of work actually implies, or where it contradicts the next one. Nothing like an integrated theory appears. The story of Harold and Erica therefore cannot illustrate a new science of human nature. It illustrates Brooks’s taste in studies. Myers adds the class objection. The happiness on offer is a dream world for people who already have the resources to turn self-knowledge into a better school, a better job, a better holiday. The unconscious, in that setting, is another accomplishment. Readers who wanted the research without the couple have often said they would have preferred a normal book of essays. Readers who wanted a novel have said the research keeps breaking the spell. Both are describing the same design.

There is a political residue, too. Brooks had made his name watching the American meritocracy with a comic eye. This book “partly refudiates” that view, Agger writes, borrowing Brooks’s own later coinage: the laurels of degrees are a false path if contentment is built underground. The refutation is softer than it sounds. Harold and Erica still succeed in recognisable ways. The unconscious is recruited to explain a life that looks, from the outside, like the life Brooks’s readers already admire. A harder book would have followed someone whose trained gut led somewhere the column could not bless.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, a great deal of mental work is not a decision you can watch yourself make, and treating people as calculators will make their choices look mad. Second, character and attachment are not soft extras beside IQ. They are part of how ability shows up in a life. Third, popular syntheses go stale where they will not sit with a contradiction, and this one often will not. Fourth, a fictional couple can make a finding memorable and can also launder it, by never letting the couple be the counterexample. Fifth, if the unconscious is most of the mind, a book about it has to risk being moved by something it did not plan. Brooks explains instead.

Read it if you want a 2011 map of the studies that were about to become dinner-party science, and you like Brooks’s voice enough to forgive the tour-guide habit. It is a poor novel and a poor textbook. It is a useful irritant if you still talk as though people choose careers, partners and politics the way they choose a phone. Nagel is right that the interesting question starts after you admit the automatic. Brooks gets you to the admission. The researchers he cites, read in their own papers, are where you have to go next.
