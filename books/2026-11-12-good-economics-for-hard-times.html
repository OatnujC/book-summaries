Abhijit V. Banerjee and Esther Duflo · 2019 · summarised 12 November 2026

Good Economics for Hard Times: Banerjee and Duflo on what the evidence will bear

Good Economics for Hard Times, published in 2019 by PublicAffairs in the United States and Allen Lane in Britain, appeared the month after Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo, with Michael Kremer, received the Nobel memorial prize for their work on experiments in development. The book is their attempt to take that habit of mind, careful evidence against a loud story, into the arguments tearing rich countries apart: migration, trade, growth, climate, welfare and the fear that machines will take the jobs. They begin with a bleak observation about why those arguments do not yield. People’s core beliefs, they write, “are better predictors of their policy views than their income, their demographic groups or where they live.” In the United States, about 5 per cent of Republicans and Democrats in 1960 said they would be unhappy if a child married outside the party. By 2010 the figure was nearly 50 per cent of Republicans and more than 30 per cent of Democrats. Identity had started to outrun circumstance. The book is a bid for a patch of common ground made of things economists can actually say.

The core argument

The core is a distrust of frictionless models. Textbooks often assume that workers, capital and migrants slide towards better opportunities until wages and prices settle. Banerjee and Duflo keep finding that people and money stick. Workers do not leave a dying town because a model says they should. Trade does not sprinkle its gains evenly. Growth, the holy object of the post-war decades, is much worse understood than the political speeches imply. “Despite the best efforts of generations of economists,” they conclude, “the deep mechanism of persistent economic growth remains elusive.” If the engine cannot be fully specified, the practical work is to reduce poverty with policies that have been shown to help particular people, rather than to wait for a theory of everything. That is the spirit of their earlier book, Poor Economics, carried into arguments that are no longer only about poor countries.

What the chapters actually find

On migration, the supply-and-demand story says a wave of low-skilled arrivals must pull local wages down. The evidence they survey, including the great European inflows to America between about 1910 and 1930, does not show that simple harm. Immigrants and local workers often do different tasks. Newcomers take jobs that need less of the local language. People already there move towards work that needs communication, including supervising. Immigrants also cluster in jobs locals avoid. A sharper natural experiment, a visa lottery in New Zealand for applicants from Tonga, found that winners roughly tripled their income within a year. The gap was the location, not a hidden difference in the people. Kemal Derviş, reviewing the book for the IMF’s Finance & Development, treats that result as support for a blunt conclusion: wage differences of that size are “caused by difference in the location and nothing else.” The political implication is awkward for everyone. Migration can transform the migrant’s life without doing the damage the headline fears, and yet the fear remains, because identity predicts the vote better than the wage table.

Trade is where they are hardest on their own profession. Ricardo’s comparative advantage still says countries gain by specialising. Stolper and Samuelson still say those gains are not shared evenly inside the country. Once you drop the assumption that capital and labour glide into the winning sector, the cheerful forecast thins. India’s liberalisation in the early 1990s did not lift poverty in a uniform mist. Some places gained far more than others. Yanis Varoufakis, writing in the Observer, credits them with saying aloud what many economists treat as heresy: the benefits of liberalisation are often modest, and the damage to weaker workers in places like the United States and India is not. He also notes the political sequel they describe. When white blue-collar men lose hope and end up on disability benefits, the old slur against “welfare queens” is no longer enough. The targets have to be redrawn as threats. Their own remedies, Varoufakis adds, stay closer to the ground than their sympathies. They are friendly to Sanders, Warren and Ocasio-Cortez, and they still recommend paying firms in declining places to keep older workers, helping people in poor countries move towards jobs, and helping those who stay to look after a village or a parent. The large stage, he complains, is left thin.

Growth gets the most philosophical chapter. From the end of the Second World War into the 1990s, rich economies grew on a run of innovations that raised the productivity of labour and capital, with a sour interval in the 1970s. The Solow model, with its diminishing returns, and Romer’s account of ideas that spill over and can defeat those diminishing returns, are both on the table. Neither, the authors think, has delivered the mechanism of growth that lasts. What they will say about the record since 1980 is distributional. Between 1980 and 2016, as one Indian review quotes them, the incomes of the bottom 50 per cent grew faster than those of the next 49 per cent, which is the part of the story that surprises people trained on rich-country stagnation. The top 1 per cent still took 27 per cent of total growth. Absolute poverty, the share of people under the global line, has halved since 1990. They refuse both the romance of automatic uplift and the claim that the poor were simply left behind.

Climate, welfare and machines are handled in the same key. Warming, they argue, will be harshest for poor countries near the equator. The line Derviş flags, that residents of North Dakota might “mostly feel perfectly happy” about a degree or two of warming, is the sort of economist’s aside that ignores storms, fires and the rest of the damage, and he says so. The ethical point underneath is sharper. The richest tenth of people produce about half of emissions. A technical fix that lets the rich keep every luxury while the poor wait for essentials is not, in their view, a solution. An LSE review summarises their preference: a carbon tax plus smaller behavioural nudges, such as clearer labels, rather than faith in green growth alone. On cash transfers, they push against the fear that help makes people lazy. On automation, they are wary of both the apocalypse and the shrug. The method throughout is the same. Find a comparison in which two similar groups met different policies or shocks, and prefer that to a story that feels inevitable.

What critics and readers push back on

Derviş admires the ambition and the modesty together. The prose is plain, the theory chapters actually contain Ricardo and Solow rather than waving at them, and the hope is that scrutinising “easy” narratives might lower the temperature. The North Dakota aside is his clearest complaint: a local comfort does not cancel extreme weather. Varoufakis’s complaint is political scale. A book this alert to damaged places still reaches for firm-level subsidies and village-level help, and he wants a larger account of the system that keeps producing the damage. Other readers find the refusal of grand growth theory unsatisfying precisely because the authors are so good at demolishing it. If nobody knows the deep mechanism, a politics that asks only for proven nudges can look like a way of never touching power, tax or the structure of firms. The randomised trials that made their name are powerful for a specific programme and awkward for a question like “what is trade for”. They know this. The book keeps placing the trials inside older theory rather than pretending a lottery in Tonga answers Stuttgart. Even so, a reader can finish it clearer about what not to believe and still unsure what a government should do on a Tuesday when the trial does not exist.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, identity now predicts policy views better than income, so throwing a wage table at a family argument will often fail. The table is still worth having, because the alternative is a panic with no numbers. Second, people do not move, retrain or reinvest as smoothly as the diagrams say, and a lot of the pain of trade and of dying industries is that stickiness. Third, migration’s largest measured effect in their evidence is on the migrant. Fourth, do not wait for a complete theory of growth before doing things that reduce poverty, and do not pretend those things are a theory of growth. Fifth, climate policy that ignores who emits, and who suffers, is a technical argument wearing an ethical blindfold.

Read it if you are tired of migration and trade being discussed as team sports, and you want the economists who won a Nobel for measurement to show their working. It helps if you can tolerate a book that is happier demolishing a false certainty than issuing a manifesto. It is the wrong book if you wanted either a socialist programme or a defence of the pre-2008 consensus. Banerjee and Duflo are at their best when a natural experiment makes a slogan look lazy. They are at their most frustrating, and most honest, when they admit that the largest economic fact of modern life, why growth persists, is still not in their hands.
