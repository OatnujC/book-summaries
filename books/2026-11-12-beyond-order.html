Jordan B. Peterson · 2021 · summarised 12 November 2026

Beyond Order: Jordan Peterson’s second dozen rules, and the voice that will not stay in the clinic

Beyond Order: 12 More Rules for Life, published in 2021, is Jordan Peterson’s sequel to the book that made him a public figure. The Guardian remembers the first as an unlikely manual, Darwin, Jung and a capital-B Being, riding a frustration with postmodern habits. The second, the same review says, continues in that vein with writing that is more laboured and arguments that are more familiar, “full of a messianic passion that can read like an unironic homage to Nietzsche,” and with too little of the psychology that would justify the heat. The Atlantic is kinder about the balance and harsher about the texture. It finds the book humbler than its predecessor, a bit more even between liberal and conservative instincts, and still a blend of the highbrow and the banal. Two Petersons share the pages. One is a clinician, talking about clients and the hard counsel he gives them. The other sounds, in the review’s image, like a student explaining that a golden snitch is a metaphor for “round chaos,” the container of a primordial element. The book matters because a large audience now takes its rules as a map. It is worth knowing which of the two voices is holding the pen.

The core argument

The rules themselves, where reviewers pause to list them, are plainer than the prose around them. The Spectator Australia hears an old-fashioned tone: do not carelessly denigrate social institutions or creative achievement; work as hard as you can on at least one thing and see what happens; plan and work to keep the romance in a relationship. The American Conservative adds the counsel to take small tasks seriously, to “have the damn fight,” and to think through how a person is socialised. Under the list is the claim that made the first book travel. Meaning is local and costly. You get it by carrying a responsibility you could have refused, inside institutions you did not design, rather than by waiting for the institutions to become worthy of you. Ideology, in the reading that review finds intriguing, lets a person skip the carrying. A system will take the blame for a failure that might otherwise have to be owned. Peterson’s public fight over compelled speech sits in the background as the example of refusing that skip. The personal and the political are, for him, the same refusal at two sizes.

The warning if you decline is written at a volume the Spectator quotes and then mocks. You will come to curse man, reality and God for the maze of obstacles. Corruption will beckon, “led as you increasingly will be by dark, unexamined motivations.” Your life, your community, your nation and the world will be impoverished, and so will Being itself. That is not a tip about making your bed. It is a sermon about the cost of drift, and the review thinks the sermon is the real book, a self-help cover on a jeremiad. Readers who already believe in work, hierarchy and tradition, it adds, do not need the rules except as a gift for a child who does not. Readers who do not believe them are unlikely to be converted by a curse on Being.

Clinic, myth, and marriage

The Atlantic thinks the clinical passages can illuminate and the mystic passages are like walking through wet sand. It quotes a sentence that shows the second habit: if the Queen turned into a fire-breathing lizard in the middle of a gala, consternation would be appropriate, but inside a story we accept it. The point, presumably, is about how narrative makes the extraordinary legible. The cost is that a reader looking for a rule has to wade. The American Conservative is more specific about where the wade happens. The first seven chapters, it says, are a dour slog. The rules are interesting and then rarely mentioned, while dense mythological analysis, repetitive and without jokes, takes the room. The second half improves. The stain on it is the chapter on marriage, which the review reads as a depressive cry about an institution collapsing, not as advice about what marriage does for the two people in it. Marriage appears as a necessary misery. You enter it or you fall into a despair that does not end. Unmarried life is sneered at, which makes the chapter, in that reviewer’s judgement, easy to dismiss.

The Spectator’s complaint about evidence is the one that travels beyond taste. Peterson, it says, often fights positions he imagines his opponents hold, rather than positions he has documented. At the Metropolitan Museum he looks at a painting, a Guido Reni by the reviewer’s guess, sees a crowd, and thinks they do not understand the symbolic meaning of the mandorla. He cannot know that. They might be scholars on an outing. The free-speech liberal in the reviewer wants to be on his side, for reading, for listening to people who know a subject, for speech against official control. The prose makes the alliance hard. “He writes terribly badly,” the piece says, and then has to argue with a man who will not let a plain duty remain plain.

What critics and readers push back on

The Guardian’s objection is proportion. A follow-up that repeats a bestseller’s passions, with less new psychology, asks the reader to treat intensity as insight. The Atlantic’s objection is mixture. The clinician is worth meeting. The explainer of primordial containers is not, and the book will not keep them in separate rooms. The conservative reviews add a political irony. A defence of order that cannot describe a marriage except as doom, or an opponent except as a caricature in a gallery, is a defence that has lost the social world it meant to protect. Fans of the first book who wanted another set of sturdy imperatives will find some. They will also find chapters in which the imperative is a pretext for a myth, and a myth that does not return them to the imperative.

None of these reviews claims the book is empty of clinical experience. Several claim that the experience is buried. That is a different criticism from the one that treats Peterson as only a culture-war brand. The brand is real. The consulting room is also real, and it is the part a hostile summary is most likely to skip. A fair reading keeps both, and then notices how often the myth is allowed to finish the paragraph the case began.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, a responsibility you can actually carry is a better unit of meaning than a system you can blame. That claim does not require the cosmology. Second, institutions and achievements can be flawed and still be worth not denigrating carelessly. Contempt is not a plan. Third, one hard piece of work, pursued past the point of mood, teaches more than a theory of work. Fourth, a rule that disappears into a myth for a whole chapter has not been given to you yet. Fifth, marriage described only as the alternative to despair is not counsel. It is a mood.

Read it if the first book helped you and you want to know whether the second voice is the same man on a worse day. Read the clinical stories and be willing to leave a chapter when the mandorla has replaced the point. It is a poor place to learn what your opponents actually think, because it often will not show you. It is a useful place to watch a gifted talker struggle to stay as concrete as his own rules. The sentence worth arguing with is the one about Being growing poorer if you drift. A life can be impoverished by drift. A cosmos does not have to be drafted in to make that true.
