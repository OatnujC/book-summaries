Yuval Noah Harari · 2015 · summarised 12 November 2026

Homo Deus: Yuval Noah Harari’s wager that humanism is a passing creed

Homo Deus: A Brief History of Tomorrow was published in Hebrew in 2015 and in English in 2016, the sequel to Yuval Noah Harari’s Sapiens. Where that book raced through seventy thousand years to explain how an unimpressive ape took the planet, this one turns around and asks what the ape will try to become next. Harari, who teaches in Jerusalem and developed the material in Hebrew from his lectures, writes in the same plain, provocative voice: large claims, a joke at the moment a historian would add a footnote, and a willingness to name a future religion before it has any churches. The English title is a prod. Homo deus, the human as a god, is not a compliment. It is a hypothesis about what people will reach for once famine, plague and war are no longer the default terrors, and about what might replace the liberal faith that each person’s feelings are the highest authority.

The core argument

Andy Ross, in a long essay on both books, puts the sequel’s claim cleanly. Sapiens told a rise. Homo Deus tells a present and a future that can only be a hostage to fortune. The thread is the rise and fall of humanism. Roughly, in Harari’s picture, humanism climbs through the Christian centuries, peaks in the Enlightenment, and begins to fail with the ideologies that treat the individual as raw material. The twilight he sketches is technological: biotechnology, computing and the engineering of bodies and minds, until intelligence comes apart from consciousness and the old punter can no longer bet on the future with any confidence. Ross hears Ray Kurzweil’s singularity in the background. He also thinks the tools are shakier than the story. An algorithm, properly, is a methodical recipe for a definite task. Harari stretches the word until organisms, desires and societies are all algorithms, which lets him compare people to machines and then find the machines wanting. The stretch is the book’s engine and its weakest joint.

The replacement faith, in the scenario that has travelled furthest, is what he calls Dataism. Ismael Al-Amoudi, reviewing the book, quotes the definition from page 367. Dataism “says that the universe consists of data flows, and the value of any phenomenon or entity is determined by its contribution to data processing.” Humans outrank other animals, on this view, only because their algorithms process more data. They are not the point of the story. They are a step, and a component, on the way to an internet of all things. The commandment Al-Amoudi quotes from later in the book is deliberately chilling: let Google and Facebook read the mail, watch the chats and keep the clicks, and “the great algorithms of the Internet-of-All-Things will tell you whom to marry, which career to pursue and whether to start a war.” A Guardian review took the same turn as its headline. In place of liberalism, democracy and the personal voice, a new religion, and the individual is not so much crushed as gently scored.

How he gets there

The route is historical, even when the destination is speculative. Harari needs the reader to feel that humanism was a successful hack, not an eternal truth. Once people could tell a story in which each human life carried an inner authority, political systems could be built on consent, markets on choice, and art on the expression of a self. The trouble, as he tells it, is that the sciences he leans on have been taking that inner authority apart. If a decision is a chain of biochemical events, each set off by the last, then the feeling of choosing is a story the system tells after the fact. Sarah Spiekermann, in a sharp blog essay, quotes him to that effect and then objects. He repeats the comparison of humans to algorithms often enough that the repetition becomes the proof. She thinks the analogy is as dated as the old claim that people work like clocks. In the technical sense descending from Shannon, information is not the same thing as meaning. Software does not, in the way a person does, permanently remake the hardware it runs on. Identity is not a file you can fetch. Calling freedom an “empty concept,” another phrase she lifts from him, only works if you have already decided that a cause behind a choice deletes the choice.

Al-Amoudi’s objection is about which humanism is being buried. The version in the book, he says, is a consumerist one: the world exists mainly to be used, an original self is waiting to be found, and immediate feelings outrank everything. Many philosophers who still call themselves humanists would reject that picture, and would point to thicker accounts of relation and responsibility. He also notices a performative problem. Why write four hundred pages on the future of humanity if humans are either shopping machines or just one algorithm among others? His charitable reading is that Harari is dissatisfied with both creeds, and wants the reader to flinch. That reading fits the tone. The Dataist commandment is written so that you will not want to obey it. Whether Harari has a third option, once he has wounded both, is less clear. Ross thinks the present-tense analysis is selective, and that a future history is allowed to be a sketch worth attacking rather than a forecast worth booking. The reader is at least given an argument with joints.

What critics and readers push back on

The algorithm talk draws the most precise fire. Geoffrey Irving, in a constructive note on both Harari books, argues that Dataism does not even survive contact with information theory. Data is not a pious substance. Shannon’s point was that you only get a usable notion of information once you separate signal from noise, and that separation depends on what you are trying to predict. “More data processing” is not a goal until someone says which distinctions matter. The total information of a physical system is not a moral improvement just because it is large. You cannot replace a dissolving humanism with a religion that comes, in his phrase, pre-dissolved. Spiekermann’s clock analogy lands in the same place from the side of experience. A rhetorically successful reduction is still a reduction. Historians, Ross adds, were already unconvinced by the breezy generalising in Sapiens. Homo Deus spends that credibility on a future, which means the usual checks, archives, rival explanations, local mess, are thinner. The book is exciting in proportion to how much it is willing to skip.

There is a political objection underneath the technical one. A story in which feelings are outdated and platforms know better is a convenient story for the firms that hold the data. Harari is not writing an advertisement, and the commandment he gives Dataism is a warning. Warnings can still teach the warned to treat the warned-against future as the only grown-up one. Readers who wanted a defence of liberal institutions, or a labour politics of who owns the models, do not get one. They get a bird’s-eye view in which those fights are already small.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, the prestige of the individual feeling is historical. It was built, and it can be demoted, which is not the same as saying it should be. Second, a society that routes more of life through prediction will be tempted to treat the prediction as the person. The marriage-and-war commandment is worth remembering because it is only a step past tools that already suggest the next song. Third, “algorithm” is a technical word, and stretching it until it covers a life makes the stretching feel like a discovery. Fourth, humanism in this book is often the consumer version. If that is the only version you recognise, the funeral will feel more inevitable than it is. Fifth, a forecast this large is a provocation. Ross is right that the interesting response is to attack the joints, not to adopt the mood.

Read it after Sapiens if you want Harari’s full arc, from foraging band to scored life, in one voice. It is a bad book to treat as a briefing on machine learning or on philosophy of mind. It is a useful book if you have noticed yourself outsourcing small judgements to a feed and you want a writer willing to say what it would mean if that habit became a civilisation. Harari is at his best when he makes a creed look like a creed, including the liberal one. He is at his worst when the next creed, data, is allowed to sound like physics. It is not physics. It is a bet, and the book is most honest when it admits the bet can be refused.
