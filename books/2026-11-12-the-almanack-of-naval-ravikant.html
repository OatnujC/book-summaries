Eric Jorgenson · 2020 · summarised 12 November 2026

The Almanack of Naval Ravikant: a decade of talks, cut into rules for wealth and for wanting less

The Almanack of Naval Ravikant, published in 2020, is not a book Naval Ravikant sat down to write. Eric Jorgenson assembled it, with Ravikant’s cooperation, from a decade of interviews, tweets and short essays, with illustrations by Jack Butcher and a foreword by Tim Ferriss. The ebook has been given away, which fits the advice inside it: media is a form of leverage, and a long game is played by being useful in public. A review that treats the format as the point, rather than as a defect to apologise for, says Jorgenson was reacting against the bloated productivity manual. The result is closer to a commonplace book than to an argument. That is why it is memorable, and why it is easy to over-trust. A sentence that survives being cut out of a conversation has not yet been asked where it fails.

The core argument

The wealth half turns on a distinction several reviewers treat as the part worth keeping. Income is what you earn by selling time. Wealth is an asset that pays when you are not in the room. The aim is not a larger salary forever. It is a point at which your time is yours, and then, ideally, a refusal to keep score past that point. The route he describes has two pieces. Specific knowledge is the skill that does not come off a syllabus: highly creative or highly technical, found by following a curiosity, hard to train into someone cheaper. If a society can train it, the same society can hire it at a lower wage. Leverage is how that knowledge stops being an hourly rate. Reviewers of the book list four kinds. Labour: other people working. Capital: money working. Code and media: the newer pair, which do not need a boss’s permission, and which can be copied at almost no extra cost. A piece of software or a recording can meet a million people while the maker sleeps. Pair the peculiar skill with one of those multipliers and you are no longer paid only for the hour you just spent.

The happiness half is less unusual in the history of philosophy and more unusual in the tech culture that quotes him. Desire is the thing to manage. A want that can be expanded without limit will outrun any asset. Stoicism, Buddhism and the startup habit of playing long games are compressed into the same register, so that a reader can hear “own equity” and “want less” as one lesson. They are not one lesson. They can be held by the same person. The almanack’s trick is to make them feel like a single temperament: patient, specific, unimpressed by status, suspicious of renting out the day.

Where the sentences fray

A criticism that takes the phrases seriously, rather than the lifestyle, says specific knowledge is a useful shove away from copycat planning and a fog when you try to use it on a Tuesday. The line, as that essay quotes him, is that it “can be found by pursuing your genuine curiosity.” If you are already winning, the edge gets the name. If you are choosing between a certificate, a better employer and a second job, curiosity is not yet a market. Markets do not pay for a private obsession. They pay when the obsession solves a problem someone can be charged for. Plenty of deeply personal skills never become income. The charitable reading is that the phrase is a searchlight, not a formula. The harsher reading is that it turns uncertainty into a style. Both can be true of the same paragraph, which is what happens when a talk is cut into an aphorism and the follow-up question is left on the cutting-room floor.

A reader on Medium tries the hourly-rate test and stalls. Ravikant suggests setting a very high figure for your time and outsourcing anything that does not clear it, so that money buys freedom rather than more chores. The reader’s objection is not that the arithmetic is wrong for a wealthy person. It is that outsourcing the unpleasant work is precisely what you cannot do if you are not already the person the advice describes. An almanack written from the far side of that threshold will tend to treat the threshold as a mood. Jorgenson does not, in the reviews’ account, stop the sentences to ask who can act on them. The form is too short for the interrogation, and the subject’s life is the only evidence on offer. One career, however thoughtful, is a sample of one.

What critics and readers push back on

The structural complaint is the one a short review puts most cleanly. The compilation rarely supplies evidence, rarely tests the idea against a case where it failed, and rarely sits with a contradiction long enough to resolve it. Repetition is inevitable once tweets and interviews are laid end to end. Rigor varies from a sharp distinction, wealth against income, to a bare assertion. The assumptions are those of a software investor in a permissionless market. A nurse, a regulated profession, a shop that cannot be copied, a person without capital or an audience, will not find code and media waiting as equal options. Calling that a tech-libertarian limit is not a sneer. It is a description of the world the sentences were spoken in. The book is most useful, that review concludes, as a provocation, not as a philosophy complete enough to live inside.

There is a smaller irony that almost every reader of this genre notices and that is still worth saying. A man with money explains that money will not make you happy, after explaining how to get the money. The happiness chapters are not thereby false. They are untested against the reader who followed the wealth chapters and remained anxious, or against the reader who never got the wealth and does not need a rich man’s permission to want less. The free download is a real choice, and it removes the easiest accusation, that the book is a funnel. It does not remove the harder one, that a gift of compressed advice borrows the authority of a life you cannot inspect.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, getting paid for time and owning something that pays without your time are different games, and confusing them keeps people busy without making them free. Second, a skill that can be trained into anyone will be priced like anyone. The valuable remainder is specific, and it still has to meet a buyer. Third, code and media can multiply work without a gatekeeper. They are not available to every kind of work, and they are not morally superior to labour. Fourth, a desire that scales without limit will eat a fortune. Wanting less is a practical art, not a consolation prize. Fifth, an aphorism from a winner is a hypothesis. It is not a result.

Read it in an afternoon if you like short sentences and you will argue with them rather than collect them. It is a bad book to treat as a career plan, and a good book to put beside a plan that is only a salary. Jorgenson’s service is the editing. Ravikant’s service is a handful of distinctions that stay in the head. The distinction to keep is wealth against income. The distinction to refuse is the quiet one between his life and yours, as if the gap were only a failure of nerve.
