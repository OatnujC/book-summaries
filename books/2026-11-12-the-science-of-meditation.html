Daniel Goleman and Richard J. Davidson · 2017 · summarised 12 November 2026

The Science of Meditation: Goleman and Davidson on what survives when the session ends

The Science of Meditation, published in 2017 and sold in the United States as Altered Traits, is Daniel Goleman and Richard Davidson trying to separate a mood from a change in the person. A review on Tiger Riding for Beginners explains the coined phrase. Plenty of things produce an altered state: a drug, a drum, an hour on a cushion. The authors want the rarer claim, a trait that is still there on an ordinary Tuesday. Davidson is known for scanning monks and yogis with tens of thousands of hours of practice. Goleman has spent a career telling general readers about emotion. Both meditate. The book is valuable because it is written by insiders who are willing to say that most of the research, including research they once trusted, does not deserve the claims made in its name. It is limited by the same fact. The people best placed to defend a hard standard are also the people whose lives are organised around the practice.

The core argument

The argument is about evidence before it is about enlightenment. A summary at Actionable Books says the authors went through something like 6,000 studies and kept about 60 they thought were methodologically sound. Many popular “truths” about meditation live in the pile they discarded. The failures they describe are familiar to anyone who has watched a fashionable intervention: practices that are not the same thing get pooled, as if a compassion exercise and a concentration exercise were one drug; samples are small; the control group is a waiting list rather than another activity taught with equal enthusiasm. When Davidson’s lab applied a stricter design and tried to remove the boost people get from being studied at all, the benefits of mindfulness-based stress reduction, that summary reports, did not look very different from a generic health programme delivered by equally keen teachers. Short courses do not, on this reading, rewrite a personality. Hours do. Different practices do different things. A trait change, where they find one, is tied to duration and to consistency.

Tiger Riding’s review walks through the middle of the book as a set of claims with boundaries. There are chapters on compassion and behaviour, on attention, on pain and on physical complaints, and on meditation inside psychotherapy. The authors keep saying that these practices were not invented as treatments. Seekers wanted a change in the quality of a life, not a protocol for an ailment. Some benefits appear anyway, outside the original aim. The review’s point is that the authors will call a spurious claim, including the kind that promises a cure or a levitation, and will also refuse the scientist’s sneer that a practice with a religious origin cannot be measured. They spend time on their own earlier naivety. That self-criticism is the tone that makes the book different from a wellness pamphlet. It is also, a sceptical reader will note, still a book that expects you to leave more interested in practice than when you arrived.

States, traits, and the monks

The distinction worth keeping is simple enough to say and hard enough to test. A state is what the session does while it lasts: a quieter mind, a shift in attention, a different relationship to a pain. A trait is what remains when you are not meditating, in an argument, in a queue, in the week after a retreat. Davidson’s extreme practitioners are the existence proof the authors lean on. If a person with an astonishing number of hours shows a brain and a temperament that look different, then a trait change is not a metaphor. The difficulty, which even friendly reviewers circle, is that those practitioners are not the customer of an eight-week course. A result that requires a monastic life does not license a promise to a commuter. The book’s honesty, when it is honest, is to keep those two populations apart. Its temptation is to let the monks illuminate the commuter with a warmer light than the short-course data allow.

A wider scientific argument, not a review of this book but the climate it was written into, makes the same demand. A 2018 paper, “Mind the Hype,” by Nicholas Van Dam and colleagues, notes that a review for the US Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality found mindfulness interventions, against active controls, only moderately helpful for anxiety, depression and pain, and weakly helpful for stress and quality of life. The authors of that paper worry about definition, about adverse effects, and about a public that has been sold a key to resilience. Goleman and Davidson are on the side of that worry more often than a bestseller’s subtitle suggests. They are not on the side of abandoning the practice. They think the real findings are narrower and more interesting than the marketing, and that the way to get them is to stop treating every cushion as the same experiment.

What critics and readers push back on

The conflict of interest is the obvious one, and it does not require a hostile reviewer to state it. Davidson has built a laboratory and an institute around this research. Goleman’s public standing is tied to the idea that inner skills can be trained. A book that discards thousands of studies can still be generous to the studies that remain in the authors’ own tradition. Friendly summaries already show the crack: once the control group is serious, a famous short programme looks ordinary. If that is the result, the monastic findings have to carry the hope, and monastic findings answer a different question. There is also the problem of the date. The book is a picture of the field as two well-placed researchers saw it in 2017. Later, larger trials have often made the effects of standard courses look modest. A reader who treats the book as the last word will be late. That last point is a caution about the field after the book, not a finding the authors could have included.

Tiger Riding thinks the technical passages will not detain most readers, and that this is a virtue. It is also how a limitation hides. “About sixty good studies” is a powerful sentence. It matters which sixty, who funded them, and whether the people in them resemble you. The book is better than the industry around it because it asks those questions of other people’s work. It is not better than a methods paper, because it still wants you to feel that a trait can change, and the feeling arrives before the caveat.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, a calm hour is not a new character. Ask what is still true on a day you did not practise. Second, meditation is not one intervention. Attention training, compassion practice and a therapy protocol should not be averaged into a single benefit. Third, an enthusiastic teacher can produce an enthusiastic result. Compare the practice with something else that is taught with the same hope. Fourth, the most striking brains in this literature belong to people who have given the practice a life, not a subscription. Fifth, the authors are participants. Their scepticism is real and it is not neutral.

Read it if you have been told that eight weeks will renovate you, and you want the case, from two believers, for a smaller claim. Read it if you teach or prescribe mindfulness and you would rather know which studies they are willing to stand behind. It will not teach you how to sit. It will teach you how to hear a result. The phrase to keep is the one in the American title. An altered state is easy to sell. An altered trait is what would have to be true after you stand up.
