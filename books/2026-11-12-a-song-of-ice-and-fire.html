George R.R. Martin · 1996 · summarised 12 November 2026

A Song of Ice and Fire: George R.R. Martin’s unfinished history of a war that thinks it is the only war

George R.R. Martin’s A Song of Ice and Fire began in 1996 with A Game of Thrones and has so far reached five novels: A Clash of Kings (1999), A Storm of Swords (2000), A Feast for Crows (2005) and A Dance with Dragons (2011). Two more, The Winds of Winter and A Dream of Spring, are still announced and still unpublished. Martin has said he came back to novels from television because the story was too large for a budget and an episode length. It has since become, by his own account and his readers’, almost too large for a hardcover. An HBO series made the title famous and then finished a version of the story the books have not finished. The novels remain the stranger object: a dynastic war told from inside too many skulls, set against a winter that the people fighting the war keep treating as somebody else’s problem.

What the five books actually do

Wikipedia’s summary of the architecture is still the cleanest. Three plots are braided. Great houses fight for Westeros after King Robert dies. The surviving Targaryens, exiled in Essos, want the Iron Throne back. In the far north, beyond a Wall of ice, the Others are a supernatural threat that the realm’s stories have filed as legend. The telling is third person, limited to one mind per chapter, a habit Martin has traced to his training in journalism. A Game of Thrones begins with nine such viewpoints. By A Dance with Dragons there are thirty-one. David Orr, in the New York Times, sorted the houses for newcomers with a bluntness the books themselves refuse: Starks as the good guys, at least one good Targaryen, Lannisters conniving, Greyjoys mostly conniving, Baratheons a mixed bag, Tyrells and Martells unclear. Lev Grossman in Time caught the method that makes the sorting fail. You experience the struggle from all sides at once, so every fight is triumph and tragedy in the same week.

The first book kills the man who looked like the hero. Eddard Stark, Robert’s friend and Hand, learns that the boy king Joffrey is the child of incest between Queen Cersei Lannister and her brother Jaime. He tries to unseat the boy and is betrayed and executed. Robert’s brothers Stannis and Renly claim the throne. Ned’s son Robb is proclaimed King in the North. Balon Greyjoy wants the Iron Islands free. The War of the Five Kings is the name the realm gives a butchery it still thinks is the main event. Jon Snow, Ned’s bastard son, takes the black on the Wall, rises in the Night’s Watch, and by the third book is entangled with Stannis, who comes north when almost nobody else will. The Red Wedding, the late chapter of A Storm of Swords that Martin has said was the last he wrote for that manuscript, is the series’ most famous refusal of the rule that a rebel king who is morally preferable gets to win. The manuscript ran to 1,521 pages without the appendices. Some publishers split it. Readers did not experience it as two books. They experienced it as a door closing.

The fourth and fifth novels are the argument. A Feast for Crows and A Dance with Dragons cover much of the same time from different people, because one volume could not hold them. The site Westeros.org reads the pair as the second act, and a second act whose climaxes point down. Characters are left at a low point. The problems are not easy, and the books do not pretend there are easy answers. A long blog reading of Dance says Martin has grown as a prose writer and that the book still meanders, and then defends the meander: he does not outline so much as follow what a person he already believes in would do, and he rewrites finished chapters when a later idea demands it. That is why a dozen travellers can be aimed at Meereen, Daenerys Targaryen’s city in Slaver’s Bay, and the logistics creak. Paste magazine notes the standing complaint that her time there is a stall until other characters arrive, and that the stall thinned a conqueror into an uncertain ruler. The television version inherited the complaint. Defenders think ruling, not conquering, was the point. The fifth book ends with cliffhangers, including a betrayal of Jon, and with two battles, one in the ice and one in Slaver’s Bay, still unfought. Martin has said The Winds of Winter was to open on those battles. As of the public record those openings are not a published book. Daenerys is not even in Meereen when Dance stops.

Why it is loved, and why the wait hurts

The love is for vulnerability. The Atlantic, quoted in the same reference summary, notes that you cannot be sure the good will triumph, which makes the moments when something like it happens feel earned. Martin gets attached to the people he kills, and he has said he loves the villains as if they were his children. Tyrion Lannister is his favourite to write, the greyest of the grey. Bran is the hardest, because he is young and because he is the door into the magic, which Martin has tried not to let eat the political novel. The “big six” he has named are Tyrion, Jon, Daenerys, Arya, Sansa and Bran. Readers write to him most about the first four. The history underneath is English more than it is a pure invention: the Wars of the Roses, the Hundred Years’ War, crusades, a duke of York and a queen who resembles Margaret of Anjou, used as flavour rather than as a code. Names repeat inside houses the way they repeat in real dynasties, which is why the book dares to have several Roberts and a trail of Brandons. You are expected to keep up.

The pain is the unfinished sentence. A reader who treats the five books as one epic, clumsily cut so it can be printed, is being faithful to how Martin describes the labour. A reader who wants the shape of a novel, with a climax, is also being faithful to what a book owes. Westeros.org suspects the next volumes, if they come, have nowhere to go but up, because the nadir has been reached. That is a hope, not a text. The show’s ending cannot be imported without lying about what Martin has published. The characters who feel most alive are alive because the plot was not allowed to drag them to a destination on a schedule. The cost of that method is a schedule that slipped from a trilogy to seven books to a silence measured in years.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, a war story that only lets you live inside the winning side is propaganda, and this one refuses the convenience. Second, the game of thrones is the smaller war. The north keeps being ignored by people who think a crown is the whole board. Third, ruling is shown as harder and duller than taking, which is why Meereen frustrates readers who wanted another conquest. Fourth, no viewpoint is safe, including the ones you were using as a compass. Fifth, an unfinished epic is still a different experience from a finished television series. If you only know the show, you do not yet know what Martin has withheld.

Read the novels if you want a long fantasy in which politics, food, law and family grievance are as present as swords, and if you can bear violence, sexual violence and the chance that the last two books never arrive. Start at A Game of Thrones and do not skip to the wedding you have heard about. It works because of the chapters that made you trust the people in the room. Stop after A Storm of Swords if you want a tragedy with a door that shuts. Continue if you want to watch a writer try to hold a world that has outgrown the object he is writing, and if you are willing to live in the low point without a promise of spring.
