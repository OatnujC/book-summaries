Jared Diamond · 1997 · summarised 12 November 2026

Guns, Germs, and Steel: Jared Diamond’s geographical answer to why some societies conquered others

In 1997 W. W. Norton published Jared Diamond’s Guns, Germs, and Steel: The Fates of Human Societies. In Britain the subtitle became A Short History of Everybody for the Last 13,000 Years. Diamond was a physiologist and ornithologist who had spent decades in New Guinea, not a historian, and the book asked a question most history departments had stopped asking: why, after the last Ice Age, did Eurasian peoples rather than anyone else end up with the tools of conquest and then expand across the world? It won the 1998 Pulitzer Prize for general non-fiction, and it is still the book many readers reach for when they want the long view of global inequality without a racial explanation. The argument is bold enough, and the objections serious enough, that it has never settled into a quiet classic.

The core argument

The book begins with a conversation on a New Guinea beach in 1972. A politician named Yali asked Diamond, in the course of a long talk about birds, volcanoes and work, “Why is it that you white people developed so much cargo and brought it to New Guinea, but we black people had little cargo of our own?” Cargo, here, means the steel tools and other products of industrial life. Diamond did not have an answer. He says it took him twenty-five years to offer one. The answer he eventually gave is that the gap had nothing to do with differences in the peoples themselves. It had to do with continental differences in the wild plants and animals that could be domesticated, which produced an earlier and richer farming package in Eurasia, and with Eurasia’s east–west axis, which let that package spread. Guns, steel, writing, states and epidemic disease are, in this account, the downstream results of food production, not the proof of a superior kind of human being.

He is explicit about the alternative he wants to shut down. Lay people, he says, filled the gap left by historians with “the transparent interpretation of supposed racial superiority”. New Guineans had struck him as curious and at least as intelligent as the Europeans he knew. In the forest he was “the dope who can’t do elementary things like follow an unmarked trail or light a fire in the rain.” If intelligence does not explain the cargo, something else has to.

From food to conquest

The mechanism has four parts, and Diamond is frank that most of the book is not a narrative history. Until about 11,000 years ago, everyone everywhere lived by hunting and gathering, at modest densities, with little stored surplus. Food production changed the arithmetic. Farming and herding yielded, in his estimate, 100 to 1,000 times more food per acre, enough to feed leaders, merchants, scribes and makers of technology. If farming had started everywhere at once, it would not explain Eurasia’s later dominance. It did not. He counts nine small regions where it arose independently: earliest, more than 10,000 years ago, in the Fertile Crescent and possibly China, and as late as 2500 BC in the eastern United States. The Fertile Crescent also got the richest suite of domesticated species. Eastern North America, New Guinea and Ethiopia got the thinnest.

Why those places, and not California or Japan? Only some species can be domesticated, and the obstacles sit in the species. It is not enough, Diamond says, to notice that kangaroos were not domesticated and conclude that they could not have been. The memorable version is the chapter the historian William H. McNeill lists as “Zebras, Unhappy Marriages, and the Anna Karenina Principle”: a domesticable large mammal needs a whole set of traits at once, and fail any of them and the attempt fails. Of fourteen valuable large domestic mammals, Diamond counts thirteen as Eurasian and one, the llama, as American. None are Australian. African antelope will not herd. Zebras bite and kick. Bison jump fences.

The plants have their own census. Of the world’s fifty-six wild grasses with the largest seeds, the geographer Mark Blumler found that thirty-two are concentrated in the Mediterranean zone of western Eurasia. Chile, California, South Africa and south-western Australia have two, one, one and none. A climate of wet winters and long dry summers favours large seeds, and western Eurasia’s Mediterranean zone is the largest and most varied of those climates.

Once farming exists, geography decides how far it travels. Crops, livestock and the habits that go with them move more easily east–west, where day length and season stay similar, than north–south, where each new band of latitude demands a new adaptation. Eurasia is wide. The Americas and Africa are long. McNeill, who admired the point, still thought Diamond leaned too hard on it: India and South-East Asia do not share Europe’s latitude, and Central Asia is a real barrier. Even so, he called it an eye-opener.

Germs are the fourth body of evidence, and the one that makes the conquests look less like battles and more like biological accidents. Europeans spread smallpox, measles and other epidemic diseases to which they had some resistance, genetic and acquired, and to which isolated peoples had none. The exchange was one-sided because most of those temperate-zone diseases came from the domestic animals people lived beside. Societies without cattle, pigs and chickens did not have an equivalent arsenal to give back. The title’s “guns” and “steel” are, by comparison, almost an afterword. McNeill thought the title odd for that reason: Diamond has little to say about guns and steel, and a great deal to say about germs and about the deeper causes underneath them.

He shows the pattern at two scales. In Polynesia, a natural experiment, people of shared origin became peaceable hunter-gatherers on the cold Chathams, where tropical crops failed, and built rank and organised war on the volcanic soils of Tonga and Hawaii. At the other scale, McNeill summarises Diamond’s account of Pizarro’s conquest of the Inca empire as a list of proximate advantages: steel, guns, horses, disease, ships, a centralised state and writing. The title is shorthand for that list. Later chapters on Australia, New Guinea and the Austronesian expansion, from south China out to Madagascar and the Pacific, struck McNeill as a revelation, including evidence that New Guinea highlanders took up food production not long after the Middle East.

What critics and readers push back on

McNeill’s review, in May 1997, grants the prehistory and then refuses the leap. The book, he wrote, is “artful, informative, and delightful”. The environmental differences are real. What he will not grant is that everything since is “a natural process of elaboration” fixed by those prehistoric gifts. People with language change their world by sharing meanings and then acting on them. He thinks Diamond treats that cultural autonomy as a residual. Diamond himself writes that local cultural habits can become “wild cards” that make history unpredictable. McNeill’s complaint is that Diamond then sets the wild cards aside.

The sharpest example is religion. Diamond describes rulers as kleptocrats who need an ideology to justify taking wealth, and a reason for strangers to die in battle so that “the whole society becomes much more effective at conquering other societies.” McNeill says a biologist who studies ants or birds might find that convincing. He does not. He also thinks the epilogue, where Diamond hopes to make human history a science “on a par with” astronomy, geology and evolutionary biology, never really sits with the surprises of recorded history. One slip McNeill flags is the claim that power shifted “irrevocably westward” under Alexander because the Middle East had wrecked its own environment, which ignores centuries of later Middle Eastern primacy.

Later critics pushed the same objection into politics. In a Bookforum essay, the historian Jackson Lears argued that a brief against racism had let imperialists off the hook. “Indeed, the word imperialism never appeared in the book. Empire, in Diamond’s view, just grew.” He quotes the anthropologist Michael Wilcox: “A more appropriate troika of destruction [than ‘guns, germs, and steel’] would be ‘lawyers, god, and money.’” On this reading, a theory that stops at geography is not a neutral science. It is a comfort, because it makes Western dominance the result of impersonal forces rather than of decisions.

Diamond’s reply, on his own website, is that none of this refutes the natural history underneath the story. He treats “geographic determinism” as a sneer rather than an argument, and he rejects the private claim, which he says he has heard from famous academics and cabinet ministers, that Europeans or Japanese are simply innately more intelligent. Germs are an embarrassing fact for an IQ theory, and so is the timeline: by 3400 BC the ingredients of power were already in place in the Fertile Crescent, and in most places, including Europe and Japan, they were imported rather than invented. An IQ story about modern Europeans would have to be, if it were consistent, a story about the ancestors of modern Iraqis and Syrians. He also says the book is more about the Fertile Crescent than about Europe. What he does concede is the question the book cannot finish: why, inside Eurasia, Europe rather than China, India or the old Near East expanded in the modern centuries. North and South Korea, he notes, diverged in sixty-five years for reasons that have nothing to do with wheat. At that scale he points readers towards later books, including Ian Morris’s Why the West Rules — For Now.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

The lessons that survive the argument are concrete. First, the largest inequalities in technology and power over the last ten thousand years are not evidence about the relative intelligence of continents. The racist answer was always a bad inference, and Diamond’s pile of natural history is still the clearest popular demolition of it. Second, farming really did change what societies could afford to specialise in, and the raw materials for farming were distributed with spectacular unfairness. Wheat, sheep and the wide east–west landmass were advantages before anyone deserved them. Third, disease was a historical force on the same scale as weapons, and it travelled with animals. Fourth, the theory is a theory of continents and millennia. It does not explain Korea, or the Industrial Revolution, or why one empire inside Eurasia broke out when others did not, and Diamond sometimes writes as if the long answer has already covered the short one. Fifth, conquest was not an automatic unfolding of biogeography. People decided to sail, to seize, to ally and to rule. A book that leaves the word for that out, if Lears is right that it does, has left out the part of the story in which anyone is responsible.

Read the full book if you want the domestication chapters, which summaries flatten, and if you are willing to hold two ideas at once: that environment shaped the possibilities, and that people still chose what to do inside them. It is a bad book to read alone. McNeill’s review, and a later historian’s account of empire, belong beside it. It is a very good book to read against the habit of explaining the modern world by blood, national character or a vague sense that some peoples were always going to be first. Diamond’s one-sentence version, the one he gives journalists, is still the right place to start. The reasons “had nothing to do with differences in the peoples themselves.” What you do with that sentence, once the ships have sailed and the laws have been written, is a question his geography cannot close.
