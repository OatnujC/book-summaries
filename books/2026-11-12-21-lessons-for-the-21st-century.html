Yuval Noah Harari · 2018 · summarised 12 November 2026

21 Lessons for the 21st Century: Yuval Noah Harari on living after the old stories fail

21 Lessons for the 21st Century, published in 2018 by Spiegel & Grau in the United States and Jonathan Cape in Britain, is Yuval Noah Harari’s present-tense book, after Sapiens took the past and Homo Deus took the future. It is a set of essays, grouped in parts, about what to do when the stories that organised the twentieth century no longer feel inevitable. Kirkus quotes the premise he keeps returning to: “humans think in stories rather than in facts, numbers, or equations, and the simpler the story, the better.” Three grand stories, in his telling, once claimed the future. Fascism was broken by the Second World War. Communism lasted a few decades more and then collapsed. The liberal story, democracy, markets and globalism, had a clear decade and then met the return of dictators, populists and nationalists. “A bit like the Soviet elites in the 1980s,” he writes, “liberals don’t understand how history deviates from its preordained course, and they lack an alternative prism through which to interpret reality.” The book is his attempt to offer one, in twenty-one goes.

The core argument

The argument a Quillette review treats as the centre is that the world is already one civilisation and still pretends it is many. Countries share a technical culture, an economic network, and, among educated people, roughly the same scientific account of life and the universe. Most of them at least say they believe in elections and a wide franchise, however often they betray the saying. The “clash of civilisations” between the West and Islam, or between liberalism and authoritarianism, is, on this view, exaggerated. What is not exaggerated is the mismatch of scale. Harari writes, in the passage the review quotes: “We now have a global ecology, a global economy, and a global science—but we are still stuck with only a national politics.” Nuclear war, ecological collapse and technological disruption “can only be solved on a global level.” Nationalism is not silly. It is the wrong size. It divides people at the moment the problems do not.

The second claim is about the liberal individual, carried over from Homo Deus and aimed at the present. Artificial intelligence, data and biotechnology could, he fears, predict relationships and careers better than the person living them. If people then hand decisions to the machine, the Enlightenment picture of a free agent starts to look like a local custom. He thinks neuroscience has already embarrassed free will among intellectuals. He worries the embarrassment will go popular, and that the social fabric which needed the story of the individual will not have a replacement. Helen Lewis, in the Guardian, lists the familiar Harari themes that sit around this worry: the pace of change, the uses and dangers of religion and of nationalism, the evil of factory farming, liberalism under threat, and the question he puts in a chapter heading. “How do you live in an age of bewilderment, when the old stories have collapsed, and no new story has yet emerged to replace them?” His older answer, that shared myths such as money and law let us build societies bigger than our biology, is still here. The secular West has faded religion from public life, and globalisation has made a coherent nation harder to believe. Bewilderment is what is left in the gap.

What the lessons try to do

Kirkus finds the essays “painfully astute” about a post-truth mood, and thinks most readers will find the narrative reasonable, including his account of why people elect the strongmen: the stories are not true, but they are rational as stories. The remedies for biotech, infotech, climate and mass unemployment “ring true,” the review says, “provided nations act with more good sense than they have shown throughout history,” which is a compliment with the escape hatch built in. Science fiction, Harari thinks, gets the future wrong partly because it insists on happy endings. Human ingenuity that already controls the outside world may soon re-engineer the inside, lengthen life and guide thought. The lessons are less a programme than a set of refusals: do not trust the simple national tale, do not trust the liberal tale to renew itself by habit, do not trust a technology story that ends in triumph because the film did.

The positive picture, such as it is, is cooperation at the size of the problem. The European Union is his favoured example of a politics that sometimes puts a wider loyalty above the nation. The Quillette reviewer finds this remarkable given Harari’s own admission that it is “far from clear whether Europe can find a middle path that will enable it to keep its gates open to strangers without being destabilized by people who don’t share its values.” Global cooperation, the review grants, will be necessary for climate and for any project of upgrading human bodies, and some countries will have to give up a short-term advantage. Pointing at the Union and stopping there is not a design.

What critics and readers push back on

That missing design is the common complaint. Quillette says the book is better at showing the weakness of nationalism than at proposing a globalism with political and economic detail, and that the gap matters because the single civilisation is the claim everything else hangs on. Dominic Sandbrook, in the Literary Review, is ruder about the politics and funnier. He quotes Harari’s introduction, in which the author says he wrestled with his conscience before criticising liberal democracy, lest the words be used to justify autocracies, and then “chose free discussion over self-censorship.” Sandbrook thinks the observations that follow are bland: equality, humility, reaching across borders, the occasional failure of democracy. He doubts Erdoğan or Putin will be either heartened or alarmed. A guru voice, he suggests, can make an ordinary decency sound like a dangerous revelation.

A sharper logical objection, from a New York Jewish Week essay, is that Harari treats the past as irrelevant to the future. If every current problem is technical, or a matter of policy, or an identity friction, then religions and older ideologies have nothing to say, and only make identity worse. The essay calls this circular. Historians can argue that laws and values, including liberal ones, were how people pushed back against domination and war. Calling them stories does not retire them. Lewis’s review is affectionate about the themes and still notices the guru problem: the same riffs, the same confidence, a reader left asking what, practically, replaces the collapsed story. The book is a diagnosis that enjoys being a diagnosis.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, people organise themselves with stories, and a simpler story will beat a true spreadsheet in an election. That is not a reason to abandon the spreadsheet. It is a reason liberals keep losing arguments they think they have already won. Second, some problems are the wrong size for a nation. Climate and nuclear weapons do not stop at a border because a speech says they should. Third, a technical system that predicts you better than you predict yourself will tempt you to hand it the decision, and the political theory built on that decision will not automatically update. Fourth, naming the European Union is not a constitution for the planet. Fifth, bewilderment is a real condition, and it is not yet a politics.

Read it if Sapiens or Homo Deus left you wanting Harari on the news rather than on the species, and if you can enjoy a clear essayist who repeats himself. It is the least argued of the three when it tells you what to build, and the most useful when it tells you why a national myth feels better than a global fact. Sandbrook is right that much of the advice is ordinary. Ordinary advice about the size of our problems is still worth having, provided you do not mistake the person offering it for a prophet.
