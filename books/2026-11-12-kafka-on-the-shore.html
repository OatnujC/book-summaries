Haruki Murakami · 2002 · summarised 12 November 2026

Kafka on the Shore: Haruki Murakami’s double myth of a boy and a man who talks to cats

Haruki Murakami’s Kafka on the Shore appeared in Japanese in 2002, as Umibe no Kafuka, and in English in 2005, translated by Philip Gabriel. John Updike, writing in the New Yorker, called it “a real page-turner, as well as an insistently metaphysical mind-bender.” He also thought it less moving than its author may have wanted, and gripping beyond its rights across more than four hundred pages. That double judgement is still the useful one. The novel is built with a strict alternation, odd chapters and even, two heroes who almost never meet in the ordinary world, and it is also willing to throw a whiskey mascot, a rain of fish and a talking cat into the same week. Readers who love Murakami love exactly that bargain. Readers who lose patience are usually objecting to the same pages.

Two stories that share a storm

The odd chapters belong to a fifteen-year-old who has named himself Kafka Tamura. He runs away from a wealthy, motherless house in Tokyo. His father, Koichi Tamura, is a famous sculptor. His mother and sister left when he was four, and he is travelling in part to find them and in part to escape a prophecy that sounds like Oedipus: that he will kill his father and sleep with his mother and sister. He carries a carefully packed backpack and, in his head, an alter ego called Crow, who speaks in bold type. Crow, Updike notes, is close to what the name Kafka means in Czech. In a prefatory chapter Crow promises a “violent, metaphysical, symbolic storm,” with “hot, red blood,” and then the line that has travelled further than the novel: “Once the storm is over you won’t remember how you made it through. … But one thing is certain. When you come out of the storm you won’t be the same person who walked in.” Kafka takes a bus to Takamatsu on Shikoku, the smallest of Japan’s four main islands, and spends his days in a private library. There he meets Oshima, a precise and learned librarian who becomes his guide, and Miss Saeki, who runs the place and carries a past the boy starts to treat as his own myth.

The even chapters belong to Satoru Nakata, whom Updike calls a mentally defective sexagenarian and whom Kirkus describes more gently as childlike and scarcely articulate. In 1944, Nakata was one of sixteen children on a mushroom-gathering walk with their teacher. A flash of something silver appeared in the sky. They lost consciousness. The others woke unharmed within hours. Nakata woke weeks later in a military hospital with his memory gone and his reading gone with it. He did not know Japan, or his parents’ faces. He learned to work in a furniture shop. When the shop ended, a government subsidy and a sideline kept him: he can talk to cats, and neighbours pay him to find the ones that stray. Cats, Updike remarks, often stand in Murakami for delegates from another world. One search takes Nakata to the sculptor’s house, where he is compelled to stab a malevolent figure in the form of Johnnie Walker, the man on the whiskey label. He flees south, hitching rides with truck drivers, towards the island where Kafka has already arrived. A young driver, Hoshino, stays with him and says at one point, in Updike’s quotation, “This is starting to feel like an Indiana Jones movie or something.”

They do not sit down together and compare notes. Updike says the two heroes interact in the realm of kami, spirits, not in the realm of plot as a realist would draw it. Nakata’s aim is almost intelligible: to return to the underworld of that childhood coma, prepared for by enormous bouts of sleep. Kafka’s aim is the curse. He hates his father enough to dream of killing him, and feels little sorrow when the father dies, but the reader, Updike notes, never simply meets the father, unless the father is Johnnie Walker. Fish and leeches fall from the sky. A figure who looks like Colonel Sanders brokers a passage into a forbidden place. There is a forest where soldiers are said to have disappeared. Oshima tells Kafka that the idea of a dream breaking into waking life is already in The Tale of Genji. The old court, in Arthur Waley’s translation, is tight enough that emotional violence looks almost naturalistic. Murakami’s version is not naturalistic. It is the same pressure, turned up until the objects in the room become omens.

Why the book is loved

The love is for the rigour inside the dream. Updike praises the “schematic rigor”: two narratives, slowly converging, each chapter a promise that the other world is still there when you turn the page. Murakami had run a Tokyo jazz club, Peter Cat, before his first novel in 1979, and the prose keeps a flat, amiable record of ordinary things, meals, records, the contents of a backpack, which makes the rupture sharper. Kirkus called the book enchantingly inventive and unusually reader-friendly, and listed the pleasures without apology: the Oedipal prophecy, the rain of things that should not be in the sky, a forest, a nod to Natsume Sōseki’s I Am a Cat. The English translation won attention that included a place on the New York Times list of the ten best books of 2005 and the World Fantasy Award. People come to it young, often as a first Murakami, because it offers a runaway, a library and a mystery, and then refuses to solve the mystery in the way a mystery novel must.

Updike thought Nakata’s story the more coherent, a holy fool on a quest, and Kafka’s the more overloaded. That split predicts the arguments. Some readers want the cat-finder’s kindness and the truck rides, and feel the boy’s sexual myth is murky in a way that is not only dream logic but a failure to imagine the women as more than stations on his curse. Others think the murk is the subject. A boy trying to outrun a story his father told him will keep meeting that story in every older woman and every act of violence, and a novel that explained the meetings would be a different, lesser book. Murakami has said in interviews, Updike notes, things that suggest he did not always know what would happen next. The suspicion while reading is that the author is finding the path a page ahead of you. For some that is freedom. For others it is a refusal to take responsibility for the symbols he has spent.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, the alternation is the method. Neither story is the explanation of the other. They are two mouths of the same tunnel. Second, ordinary detail is not a rest from the myth. The backpack and the bus timetable are how the myth stays believable long enough to hurt. Third, Nakata’s goodness is not wisdom. It is a mind emptied by an event the state never properly named, and the novel treats that emptiness as a kind of vocation. Fourth, Kafka’s curse is a warning about stories that tell a child what he will become. Whether he fulfils it is less clear than his fear that he already has. Fifth, you are allowed not to understand the ending. Updike did not pretend the book was moving in proportion to its grip. Coming out of the storm not the same is the promise Crow makes. Remembering how you made it through is the promise he withholds.

Read it if you want a long novel that is genuinely strange and still a page-turner, and if you can tolerate sex, violence and a metaphysics that will not file a report. It is a bad book to read for a single allegory you can explain at dinner. It is a very good book if you have ever felt that a dream continued after you opened your eyes, and you wanted the daylight to admit it. Start with Nakata if the boy exhausts you. Stay for the library. The storm, if Murakami keeps his word, will not leave you with a tidy moral. It will leave you slightly rearranged, which is the only ending Crow was willing to swear to.
