Robert Greene · 2012 · summarised 12 November 2026

Mastery: Robert Greene’s claim that genius is a long obedience, not a gift

Robert Greene’s Mastery, published in 2012, is the softer sequel to books about power, war and seduction. The subject this time is craft. Kirkus puts the thesis bluntly: genius can be learned if we pay attention and reject social conformity. Fortune describes the goal as finding a Life’s Task and then walking a path from student to practitioner to master, until the work yields both achievement and a sense of fulfilment. The book is a gallery of lives, Einstein, Darwin, Mozart, Temple Grandin among them, arranged so that a reader can see the same sequence in a laboratory, a studio and a workshop. It matters because a great many ambitious people are told that originality comes first. Greene thinks originality is what you are allowed to attempt only after you have swallowed a field whole, and that the person who cannot read a room will lose the chance to practise at all.

The core argument

The path has three phases that several readers of the book describe in the same order. First an apprenticeship, in which you absorb what the field already knows, under people who know it better. Then a creative-active phase, in which you experiment, combine, and find a voice that is yours. Then mastery itself, an intuitive command that feels like instinct and is in fact depth. The mistake, on this account, is to skip the first phase and call the result original. What you get is novelty without the knowledge that would make a contribution. Greene’s own earlier reputation was for strategy of a colder kind. Here the strategy is pointed at a vocation. Fortune notes that he treats mastery as interdependent rather than solitary. Social intelligence, in the line the magazine quotes, is “the ability to see people in the most realistic light possible,” instead of projecting your needs onto them. “Success attained without this intelligence is not true mastery and will not last.” A mentor has to be absorbed and then left. A rival has to be seen clearly. Naivety about motives, he thinks, wastes talent as surely as laziness does.

Under the careers sits a story about the species. Kirkus summarises it: frontal vision and fine hand-eye coordination let early humans pause and consider alternatives, and mirror neurons let them guess what another animal, or another person, was about to do. For most of the time since, we were hunter-gatherers, and the brain, Greene suggests, is built for that pace. A technological society rewards the quick judgement. Mastery is the refusal of that reward. You stay with one skill until tedium has done its work, and only then do you vary it. A Los Angeles Times review hears the echo of Malcolm Gladwell’s 10,000 hours, and Fortune says Greene’s recommended apprenticeship lasts about that long, a figure others had already treated as a tipping point. He adds a vocation, a serious mentor, and a social network. Hours alone, in his version, do not select the work.

What the examples are doing

The examples are the book’s method and its vulnerability. Kirkus calls them interesting and tendentious. Darwin is the case the review stops on. Greene’s telling, it says, leaves out the intellectual influences that shaped the theory, including geological thinking Darwin already knew before the voyage that the popular story treats as the beginning. A life rearranged to fit a phase is still a life, but it is no longer evidence that the phase produces the life. Temple Grandin is used, in the same review’s account, as a model for building a social mask when ordinary social reading fails. That is a specific story about autism and work. Generalising it into a rule that everyone should wear a deceptive face is a different claim, and Kirkus thinks Greene makes the leap. Mozart and Einstein are there because readers already believe in them. A method that can be illustrated by anyone famous is a method that has not yet been tested against the people who followed it and stayed obscure.

The social chapters are where the older Greene shows through. Readers who come from The 48 Laws of Power will recognise the attention to manipulation. The integration is deliberate if you accept his premise that a dreamer who ignores the human side of a craft rarely gets the years the craft requires. It is also the part that makes the book feel less like a hymn to vocation and more like a manual for surviving institutions. Both readings can be true. A laboratory and a studio are political places. Pretending otherwise is how talented people get used.

What critics and readers push back on

Fortune’s practical objection is about age. An apprenticeship of 10,000 hours is a young person’s plan, or a person who can afford to start again. Many of the readers who buy a book like this are already in the middle of a career, with dependants and a reputation in the wrong field. Telling them to submit to a decade of observation is advice that assumes a freedom the book does not discuss. Kirkus’s intellectual objection is about the history. If the Darwin chapter can omit the ideas Darwin actually inherited, then the other chapters may be doing the same favour to Mozart and Einstein. A pattern found by selecting the details that fit is not a law of development. It is a story with a moral.

There is a third tension inside the thesis itself. Greene wants you to listen to an inner voice and to reject conformity, and he also wants you to spend years obeying the rules of a field and the moods of a mentor. The resolution he offers is sequential: obey first, deviate later. That is sensible as a sequence and slippery as a test. Almost any career can be narrated afterwards as obedience followed by a break. The book is at its best when it slows a hurried reader down. It is at its weakest when the slowing is justified by a biography that has been tidied.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, original work that has not absorbed the field is usually a costume. The apprenticeship is the unfashionable part, and it is the part most worth hearing. Second, one skill, practised past the point of boredom, is a better foundation than five skills sampled for pleasure. Third, the room is part of the craft. Seeing people as they are, rather than as you need them to be, protects the hours you meant to spend learning. Fourth, a famous life is not a proof. Ask what the telling left out. Fifth, ten thousand hours is not available to everyone who is called, and a guide that ignores that is a guide for the unencumbered.

Read it if you are early enough in a field to use a long apprenticeship, or if you keep abandoning work at the first dull month and want a case for staying. Read the social chapters with the earlier books in mind, so you notice when craft slides into manoeuvre. It will not make you Darwin, and the Darwin in it is not quite Darwin. It will make a hurried person harder to fool about what depth costs, which is a smaller promise and a more honest one.
