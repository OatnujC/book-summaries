Michael Pollan · 2018 · summarised 12 November 2026

How to Change Your Mind: Michael Pollan’s cautious trip through a science that cannot be blinded

Michael Pollan’s How to Change Your Mind, published in 2018, is a food writer arriving late to a class of drugs he had mostly declined. The Guardian quotes the joke he makes at his own expense: “The only way I was going to get to Woodstock was if my parents drove me.” He was, the review says, a bit too late and a bit too square for the first psychedelic era. Then, in the 1990s, American laboratories began to reopen the question, quietly, for the dying, for people with alcohol problems, and for depression that had not answered to the usual treatments. The book is a history of that opening and closing and opening again, and a travelogue of a middle-aged man taking LSD, psilocybin and a toad’s venom under supervision. It matters because the news had started to write itself: a dad joke about hippies, then a claim that a forbidden drug might be medicine. Pollan is the respectable narrator that claim needed. The difficulty, which he does not hide and which his best critics will not let drop, is that the experience depends on the room, the guide and the expectation, and a medicine that cannot be separated from those things sits badly inside the trial design we use to trust medicines.

The core argument

Pollan’s history, as Mike Jay reconstructs it in the London Review of Books, follows a story the researchers like to tell. In the 1950s the drugs “exploded” in the West and early trials looked promising. In the early 1960s exuberance became moral panic. Timothy Leary’s “antics,” in the version Pollan summarises, lit “a public bonfire” of hard-won knowledge. By the 1970s the substances were outlawed and underground. Only recently, the story goes, has the hostility thinned enough for the therapeutic potential to be grasped properly, and for the first time. Jay thinks that plot is too clean, and that psychiatrists and patients are largely missing from the international scene now selling it. What Pollan does capture, and Jay grants him, is the practical discovery the early therapists made. People on these drugs are highly suggestible. The CIA noticed, and looked at LSD as a possible tool of brainwashing. The therapists noticed too, and built what later got called set and setting: the patient’s expectations, and the room. Al Hubbard and Humphry Osmond avoided a sterile ward. Music played. There were books of art. Osmond arranged something of that kind for Aldous Huxley’s first mescaline experience, the one behind The Doors of Perception.

The modern chapters turn on a quality Pollan borrows from William James. The experience is noetic. People feel, in James’s description as Jay quotes it, that they have been “let in on a deep secret of the universe, and they cannot be shaken from that conviction.” Pollan tries LSD, mushrooms, ayahuasca and the secretions of the Sonoran desert toad, and writes the sessions without, an anaesthesiology reviewer notes, the prose that usually makes clinicians close the book. On mushrooms he asks a guide to turn off the spa music and put on Bach’s cello suite in D minor. Jay quotes the result: even to call it music “is to diminish what now began to flow, which was nothing less than the stream of human consciousness, something in which one might glean the very meaning of life and, if you could bear it, read life’s last chapter.” Later the doubt returns, in Pollan’s own phrase, “Fool, you were on drugs!” What remains is that he did experience it. He decides the molecules may be wasted on the young, once “the cement of our mental habits” has set. What they offer can sound like a greeting card. He thinks the power is not cancelled by the banality. “The mind is vaster, and the world ever so much more alive, than I knew.”

Therapy, and the trial that will not sit still

The clinical hope is that a session or a few sessions might do what a daily pill does not. Jay notes the claim as Pollan and others frame it: not a lifetime of maintenance, but a short course, sometimes a single sitting, with effects that outlast the drug. The mechanisms a clinical reviewer lists are psychological rather than a simple receptor story. People feel less sealed off from nature and so less afraid of rejoining it. Addiction’s broken links look restorable from outside the craving. A tyrannical story about the self can be stepped out of. Pollan calls the drugs ego-lytic, a reboot when ordinary care has failed. Rick Doblin of MAPS tells him, in Jay’s extract, that the medical paradigm is also “a means to a more ambitious and still more controversial end”: psychedelics in American culture, not only in the clinic. The veterans’ PTSD trials were chosen in part because a marriage cannot be called a disease, and a soldier can. At a MAPS conference, Tom Insel, lately of the National Institute of Mental Health, advises the movement to drop the language of revolution and say “psychedelic-assisted therapy.” Pollan calls even that hybrid “a very large pill for modern medicine to swallow.”

The pill is large because the method and the drug are the same object. Jay puts the clash cleanly. Set and setting are what a therapist arranges. Expectancy effects and confounding variables are what a trial designer fears. A placebo with no psychoactive effect is spotted at once. A placebo with the same mental effect is no longer a control. After thalidomide, American law demanded reproducible efficacy against placebo. Psychedelic responses vary, which made the drugs look, under that definition, like the opposite of a quality product. A clinical reviewer of Pollan’s book says the same thing without hostility. Studies cannot be adequately blinded, and the effect, good or bad, is greatly influenced by set, setting and expectation. Pollan, the reviewer thinks, does not proselytise even after he has been converted, and he will call a crackpot while still taking the mystical report seriously. The limit is not a failure of his reporting. It is the phenomenon.

What critics and readers push back on

Jay’s sharpest point is about who gets the trip. The first Johns Hopkins psilocybin trial recruited through small ads asking who was “Interested in the Spiritual Life?” That is not a mental-health service user. Pollan moves through clandestine guides, meditation, depth psychology, neo-shamanism. Lauren Slater, in the contrasting book Jay reviews beside him, asks for MDMA and is told it will not mix with her antidepressant, then is told by an underground guide that a psychiatric history means it will not work for her. She does not get a session. A short essay on elite framing makes the historical version of the same complaint. A story of doctorates, government insiders, film stars and Silicon Valley leaves out how most people have actually met these drugs. Ketamine, already prescribable, was being offered in California, Jay reports, at hundreds of dollars a dose that cost the clinic a couple of dollars. If the medicine follows the money, the worried well will be treated and the people the headlines invoke will wait.

There is a political story under the scientific one, and Pollan includes the man who says it aloud. Doblin wants prescription status on the way to a wider cultural incorporation. That is a legitimate ambition and a reason to read every “breakthrough” headline twice. The underground guide who stopped seeing difficult clients after a lawsuit is the other half. Risk is being managed by excluding the people for whom the risk is the point of the research.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, these experiences are unusually suggestible, which is why a guide and a room can make a revelation or a horror out of the same molecule. Second, a trial that cannot be blinded is still information, and it is weaker information than a trial that can. Third, a benefit that lasts after the drug has left is a different medical object from a pill you take every morning. Fourth, the people in the spiritual-life advert are not the people on a psychiatric ward. Do not let one result stand for the other. Fifth, a middle-aged sceptic who changes his mind is a good witness and a narrow sample.

Read it if you want the history and the sessions in one voice, and if you can enjoy a careful enthusiast. It is the wrong book if you want a protocol, a dose, or permission. Pollan is explicit that he was looked after. Jay is right that being looked after, in this literature, is not a side detail. It is the treatment. The line to keep is his own, about the cement of habit. The line to argue with is the movement’s, that the bonfire is over and the science can now be simple. The science is interesting precisely because it will not simplify.
