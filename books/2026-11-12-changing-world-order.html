Ray Dalio · 2021 · summarised 12 November 2026

Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order: Ray Dalio’s case that empires rise and fall on a Big Cycle

In November 2021, Avid Reader Press, an imprint of Simon & Schuster, published Ray Dalio’s Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order: Why Nations Succeed and Fail. Dalio is the founder of Bridgewater Associates, the hedge fund he built into the world’s largest, and this is the book in which he tries to do for countries what he had already tried to do for a life and a portfolio: turn a messy history into a set of repeatable principles. His own site describes the project as an examination of “history’s most turbulent economic and political periods”, meant to show why the years ahead “will likely be radically different from those we’ve experienced in our lifetimes—but similar to those that have happened many times before.” The book still matters because it is one of the few popular accounts that puts debt, domestic fracture and great-power rivalry in the same frame, and because a lot of powerful people have treated that frame as a map. Henry Kissinger called it “a serious contribution and an urgent warning.” Larry Summers called it “essential reading to understand our times.”

The core argument

Dalio’s claim is that the rise and fall of the leading powers over the last five centuries is not a string of unique accidents. It is a Big Cycle, made of three smaller ones that move together: a cycle of debt, money and the economy; a cycle of order and disorder inside a country; and a cycle of peace and war between countries. His chart appendix lines the determinants up in a sequence. A rising power tends to improve education first, then innovation and technology, then competitiveness, military strength, trade and economic output, then the depth of its financial centre, and at last the status of its currency as a reserve that other people want to hold. Decline, in his telling, runs the film backwards. Debts get large, money is printed, wealth gaps widen, internal conflict sharpens, leadership weakens, and eventually the reserve currency is lost.

He is not saying the timetable is a law of physics. On CNN he has described the stages as predictable but not inevitable, and the book is full of the provisos of a man who makes his living being a bit more right than the market. The practical point is still a stern one. The post-1945 American-led order, with the dollar at its centre, looks to him like a late stage of a cycle he has seen before, in the Dutch Republic and then in Britain. China’s rise looks, on the same gauges, like an earlier stage. The investor’s job, and the citizen’s, is to notice which stage you are in before the headlines make it obvious.

How the cycle is supposed to work

The historical spine is the succession of reserve currencies. Reviewing the book for the National Business Review, Nevil Gibson traces Dalio’s thread from 1600: the Dutch guilder, then British sterling and the gold standard, which in this account lasts, with interruptions, from the American Civil War until its end in 1971, and then the US dollar. Dalio treats each handover as the visible tip of a deeper change in who can borrow cheaply, who sets the rules of trade, and who can fight a war without going broke. Along the way, he says, the reserve currencies pass through repeated devaluations and credit crises. Prosperity sags, the system is patched, and the cycle starts again with a new leader or a diminished one.

Inside a country, his charts sketch six stages. A new order arrives with new leadership. Systems for allocating resources, and the bureaucracies that run them, get built and refined. Peace and prosperity follow. Then come the excesses: widening gaps in wealth and other things people fight over. Financial conditions turn bad and conflicts intensify. At the far end are civil war or revolution, a breakdown of the currency, and, often at the same time, an external war that settles who writes the next set of rules.

The measures he uses are deliberately blunt, because he wants something he can score. Education, inventiveness, competitiveness, the share of world trade, military strength, the size of the financial centre, and how much of the world’s transactions, debts and central-bank reserves are denominated in the currency. He applies the same dashboard backwards. That is where the book is most interesting, and most honest about its own limits. Looking at the eighteenth century with the indicators he uses on modern economies, he writes: “I can now see what the indicators would have shown at the time … and I can [also] see from that, that the picture would not have been crystal clear that the British Empire would have gone on to become the dominant world empire.” Gibson reports that Dalio would instead have favoured the Dutch to keep their lead, and would have rated Bourbon France as a major power on the way up. Both then faded, the French monarchy overthrown in the revolutionary years and the Dutch, in this telling, failing to turn early commercial success into industrial power. Britain surged anyway. The method, applied honestly, does not always pick the winner in advance.

He dates Britain’s own turn towards decline from about 1900, when a united Germany was rising and inequality at home had become extreme. Gibson summarises one of Dalio’s figures as 1 per cent of the population owning 74 per cent of the wealth. Two world wars then finished the sterling order. Europe today, Dalio writes, offers little comfort to anyone hoping for a third Western centre: “Europe’s debt is large, its economy is fundamentally weak, its internal conflicts are relatively large, its vitality and levels of inventiveness are relatively weak, and its military is not strong.” Populism, he adds, has fed on that inequality. Gibson notes that the observation sat awkwardly well beside the pressure Russia was already putting on Ukraine when the review appeared in January 2022.

China is the contentious half of the dashboard. On Dalio’s cycle, a country that has already lived through several dynastic collapses is now well into peace, productivity and the building of power, while the United States is further along the path of debt, internal conflict and relative decline. He expects the renminbi to matter more as a reserve currency, and he treats China’s military expansion as the usual companion of economic weight. He also asks Americans dealing with Beijing to look at the relationship from the Chinese side, and he plays down the distance between state-backed capitalism and the Western kind. The differences, in his view, are smaller than the similarities. On Taiwan he is coldly instrumental: a pledge to fight a war over the island is, in Gibson’s paraphrase of him, not worth a shooting war. On Hong Kong he is brief. The Chinese public, he believes, will not be argued into thinking Western democracy is obviously better than the stability they have.

Underneath the geopolitics is the investor’s moral, and it is narrower than the book’s size suggests. Non-cash assets, property and shares, are the cushions he trusts against inflation, debt crises and bad political decisions. History is useful only insofar as it improves the bet. “For an investor,” he writes, “understanding history accurately is of no use relative to being a bit more right or wrong about the future.” Key sentences are set in bold so a hurried reader can skim. An appendix scores the prospects of the leading economies. The whole machine is built for someone who has to decide, not for someone who wants the past left in its own tangle.

What critics and readers push back on

The endorsements on Dalio’s site are from the world the book is written for: Kissinger, Summers, Hank Paulson, Graham Allison, Jamie Dimon, Tim Geithner. Andrew Ross Sorkin calls it “a provocative read” and credits Dalio with “metrics from history that can be applied to today.”

The objections are about what a dashboard leaves out. Gibson, who likes the charts from 1600 onwards and thinks they help an investor be “more likely to be right than wrong”, still calls the explanations simplistic. Earlier Principles books, he notes, offered self-improvement without much ethics or philosophy, and this volume has the same habit. Similarities between Marx’s communism and modern capitalism are drawn too cleanly. Climate, both the warming kind and the older rhythm of warm and cool periods, barely enters a story about why economies rise. Psychology, anthropology and archaeology are thin. A cycle “as regular as the tides”, which is Gibson’s image for Dalio’s prosperity and depression, will always look tidier than the record once you know who was choosing, resisting and being crushed.

China is where that tidiness does the most harm. Gibson argues that Dalio’s enthusiasm, and his high-level connections, including a son partly educated in China, sit beside a mild account of the Communist Party’s system. Set next to a London critic’s list of overinvestment, weak corporate accountability, expropriation, thin legal protection, corruption and unreliable company reporting, Dalio’s reservations look small. A reader can accept the warning about American debt and division and still think the book flatters the rising power it is most eager to understand. There is also a deeper quarrel about money itself. The reserve-currency story is powerful from the Dutch Republic onwards, but it is a modern arrangement. Treating it as the timeless engine of empire flattens earlier centuries, and it can make printing money sound like the same act in 1700 and in 2008. Dalio knows the stages are not fate. The charts, with their clean rise and decline, keep tugging the reader towards fate anyway.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

The useful residue is smaller than the book, and sturdier. First, watch the order of things, not the headline of the moment: education and inventiveness tend to show up before a currency becomes someone else’s savings, and debt, division and money-printing tend to show up before that privilege frays. Second, treat reserve-currency status as a political fact, not a law of nature. The guilder gave way, sterling gave way, and the dollar’s ease since 1971 is a phase, not a permanent feature of the world. Third, remember Dalio’s own eighteenth-century test. The same indicators that look decisive in hindsight would not have made Britain’s rise obvious at the time, and would have flattered powers that then declined. A model that confesses this is more trustworthy than one that does not. Fourth, separate the American warning from the China forecast. High debt, a wide wealth gap and bitter internal conflict are worth taking seriously even if you reject his relatively gentle reading of Beijing. Fifth, notice what the book is for. “Being a bit more right or wrong about the future” is an investor’s standard. It is a poor standard for history, and a dangerous one for policy, if being a bit right means talking yourself out of a war or into a complacent view of a rival.

The full book is worth reading if you work with markets, public finance or great-power risk and you want the charts and the scoring system rather than a paraphrase of them. It is also worth it if you have absorbed a lot of postwar American optimism and would like that optimism argued with, in one place, by someone who has to put money on the argument. It is a weaker use of time if you already read history for its exceptions, or if you want China described by people who do not have a stake in being welcome there. Dalio is at his best when he shows you a measure and then admits it would have misled him. He is at his worst when the measure becomes a story about what other societies are willing to live with. Read him for the dashboard. Do not let the dashboard choose your politics.
