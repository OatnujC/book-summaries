Bill Gates · 2021 · summarised 12 November 2026

How to Avoid a Climate Disaster: Bill Gates on the extra cost of doing it clean

Bill Gates’s How to Avoid a Climate Disaster, published in 2021, is an engineer’s book with a politician’s title. The New Republic describes the scope he actually chose. The world needs to shrink what he calls green premiums, the extra cost of a no-carbon way of doing a thing compared with the dirty way of doing it. The zero-carbon options worth deploying at scale, he writes, are those “with no premium at all.” Everything else, in this framing, is a programme to get the premium down: invention, demonstration, a market that can then buy the clean version because it is no longer a luxury. Reuters thinks the answers themselves are familiar to anyone who watches the subject. A carbon price. Electrify what can be electrified, with energy that does not add carbon. Governments spending heavily so that the remaining problems, the ones electricity will not solve, get a technology rather than a slogan. The book’s service is to lay the size of those problems out so a non-specialist can see which parts are already cheap and which parts are not. The argument it starts, and does not finish, is whether a premium is the right description of a fight that is also about power.

The core argument

Gates thinks, in a line MIT Technology Review quotes, “more like an engineer than a political scientist.” An engineer looks for the process that is still too expensive or too rare: cement, steel, aviation fuel, the winter night when the wind drops, the carbon that must be pulled back out of the air because some emissions will not reach zero in time. Most pathways that keep warming near one and a half or two degrees, the New Republic notes, already assume a cheap way to do that removal. Gates’s insistence that innovation is not optional is, on that point, hard to wave away. His further insistence is the one critics call a deflection. In a Rolling Stone interview the magazine’s reviewer quotes, he says the value is to take the world’s innovation power and “bring green premiums down by 95 percent. That’s what the U.S. has to do. All the other stuff is just so much confusion.” The other stuff, in the review’s telling, is the work that does not wait on a breakthrough: rebuild the grid, build the clean supply that already exists, and cut the fossil production that the new machines are meant to replace.

Reuters is useful on what the premium is and is not. It is a comparison that depends on a guess about how fast a cost will fall. Wind, solar and batteries have fallen fast enough to embarrass older guesses, which is why a premium written down in one year can look quaint in the next, and why a premium that assumes the same luck for a harder industry can be a wish. The book is at its clearest when it separates sectors. Cement, steel and aviation fuel are not the same problem as a power grid that can already be rebuilt with machines that exist. A reader who leaves with that separation, and with a feel for which premiums are already small, has got the part of the book that other explainers often skip. A reader who leaves thinking the remaining premiums are a reason to wait has got the part the author sometimes feeds.

What the tool leaves out

Molly Scott Cato, quoted in Raconteur, likes the instinct to use a price and to ask how much wellbeing you can get from a given amount of energy. She thinks the tool is too sure that what it can measure is what it can manage. Planetary systems are not a product line. Biofuel is her example of a displacement the premium does not see: the fuel looks cleaner in the tank and the question of whether a peatland or a food field was spent to grow it sits outside the cell in the spreadsheet. Oliver Bolton, in the same piece, adds that businesses will not run their decisions on green premiums until governments change the policy that makes the dirty option rational. Jane Davidson’s objection is blunter. She is disappointed by the proposition that green is more expensive. Each year of not being green has a cost of its own, and calling the clean choice a premium trains people to treat the damage as the default price and the repair as the extra.

MIT Technology Review pushes the political version. Gates does not, the review says, spend much time on the interests that block a technology even after it is cheap. Climate-change denial is strangely absent. The Koch brothers, whose fortune was petrochemical and whose role in organised denial is a matter of record, are the connection the review thinks an engineer ought to make if he is going to throw up his hands at polarisation. Feedback in a complex system is not only technical. A cheaper solar panel does not automatically retire a lobby. The New Republic calls the result a shiny-object politics. Innovation is easy to applaud. The heavier lifting is dull, local and immediate, and a book that keeps pointing past it gives cover to anyone who would rather fund a laboratory than close a plant.

What critics and readers push back on

There is a second line of attack, from people who think the diagnosis itself is oversold, and it should not be confused with the first. A review in European Scientist accepts the practical energy chapters and rejects the urgency, the treatment of every extra tonne as a tonne that must be avoided, and the habit of adding methane and nitrous oxide to the carbon bill. That is a quarrel with the climate science Gates takes as given, not a quarrel with his engineering. The more durable objections do not need that quarrel. They say the premiums are real, the breakthroughs for a few sectors are real, and the book’s title promises an avoidance strategy that its pages only partly contain. Reuters is the friendliest of the serious reviews and still will not call the sums unanswerable. A cost curve is not a coalition.

Gates is an investor in many of the technologies a book like this ends up recommending. The reviews I am using do not make that the centre of the case, and it would be lazy to pretend a financial interest empties a technical argument. It does change how a reader should hear “breakthrough.” A man whose fortune and whose climate work both sit in early technologies will see the missing machine more vividly than the machine that is already on the grid. The book is the product of that eyesight. It is good eyesight for cement and aviation. It is partial eyesight for a decade in which the argument is about deployment.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, “clean” is not one number. Ask the premium sector by sector, and do not let a hard sector veto an easy one. Second, some emissions will not be engineered away by electrifying the obvious, and those need a research programme that is allowed to fail. Third, a premium that ignores a peatland, a grid queue or a fossil lobby is an incomplete price. Fourth, waiting for a 95 per cent reduction in every premium is a way to miss the cuts that do not need it. Fifth, the author tells you he thinks like an engineer. Believe him, and then go and read a political account of the same decade.

Read it if you want a clear map of where clean options are still painfully expensive, written by someone who has spent money trying to make them less so. It is a poor book if you want the social bargain, the workers, the denial industry or the case for stopping a project that is already profitable and dirty. Those are the “other stuff” he called confusion. They are also the reason a premium can fall on paper and the plant can keep running. Use the map. Do not let the map tell you the territory is only a laboratory.
