Christian Rudder · 2014 · summarised 12 November 2026

Dataclysm: Christian Rudder’s claim that the click is a truer self than the sentence

Christian Rudder’s Dataclysm: Who We Are (When We Think No One’s Looking), published by Crown in 2014, is a founder of OkCupid turning the site’s logs into a portrait of desire, race, age and bias. The pitch is that people lie in surveys and tell the truth with a click. When they describe what they do, they idealise. When they do not know they are in the experiment, they reveal what Rudder wants to call universals. “Practically as an accident,” he writes, in the line the Los Angeles Review of Books quotes, “digital data can now show us how we fight, how we love, how we age, who we are, and how we’re changing.” The accident is the part a privacy scholar will not let stand. Someone chose to take messages sent in hope of a date and publish them as a theory of the species. The book matters because that choice became normal, and because some of the patterns he charts are ugly enough that the method’s flaws do not make the patterns disappear.

The core argument

Rudder’s claim is epistemic before it is moral. Self-report is a poor instrument for prejudice, attraction and status, because the person being asked is also the person who wants to look decent. Behavioural data, collected at a scale no lab can match, is the better instrument. OkCupid users rate photographs, answer match questions and write to strangers. From that he draws charts: who gets messages, who replies, how age changes the pool, how race cuts across the ratings people will not admit to. A Globe and Mail review picks out the findings that travelled. Men, in his data, lose interest in women past the very early twenties. Looks matter more in the first click than they seem to matter once two people are actually sitting down: “people appear to be heavily preselecting for something that, once they sit down in person, doesn’t seem important to them.” And users who say they are open turn out, in the aggregate, “massively biased toward our own race,” with white profiles coming second for many non-white users. The same review notes a labour-market sting. Attractive women receive more job opportunities in the data he examines, while a man’s looks barely move his. If women are hired for a trait unrelated to the work, their later failure rate will be higher for that reason alone. “It’s a problem the Internet is surely making worse,” because the picture is now attached to almost everything.

The charts, and the sentences around them

Race is the chapter that made the book famous and that Cathy O’Neil, writing as mathbabe, thinks he botches by bluntness. Men of various races rate women’s photos. Non-black men, in the chart, prefer their own group and rate black women lower. Rudder then shows that other American dating sites look similar, and that the pattern does not hold in the same way outside the United States. O’Neil says that last fact, the one that complicates an American story, is buried at the end. What sits next to the opening chart is a match question: would you date someone who has voiced a strong negative bias against a race? Eighty-four per cent say no. Rudder’s punchline, which she quotes, is that in the light of the ratings “84 percent of people on OKCupid would not consider dating someone on OKCupid.” She finds this careless. A preference in a photograph is not the same act as announcing a hatred, and calling the gap hilarious risks emptying the word racism. She also thinks he had the data for a careful chapter and did not write it. Users are trained by the product to rank. Turning around and prosecuting them for ranking is, she says, creepy, and he never fully comes back from that tone.

Stephen Few, a visualisation critic, attacks the missing context rather than the politics. When Rudder talks about the age of man a woman finds most attractive, Few cannot tell whether the data are choices between photographs, messages sent, or something else. Scientists have to describe the design. Without it the chart is a mood. The same objection hits the famous curve in which messages shoot up for the most attractive few per cent of profiles. How was attractiveness measured? A word list that is “most distinctive” of white men, or of black women, is interesting as a list of phrases used more by one group on a dating site. It is not, Few says, a portrait of the group, because the method throws away the words everyone uses. Self-description while flirting is a context. Rudder often talks as if the context had evaporated and the essence remained.

The age line is the one reviewers quote when they want to show the book’s swagger. Rudder writes, in the LA Review’s extract, “A woman’s at her best when she’s in her very early twenties. Period.” Women, in the same findings, want partners who age with them. Men keep aiming young. The Globe and Mail thinks you might have guessed the twenty-three cutoff without a dataset, and that other results are surprising enough to change behaviour. The sentence with “Period” is doing something else. It turns a skew in one site’s messages into a fact about women’s value. That is the move the book keeps making, and the move its best critics refuse.

What critics and readers push back on

O’Neil’s carelessness charge and Few’s context charge meet in a third objection from the LA Review. What Rudder calls an accident, data revealing a racial pattern people deny, is a decision to take information out of the setting it was given in and use it again. Privacy scholars call that secondary use. The users were trying to get a date, not to enrol in a demography of the soul. A later note on the race charts adds technical doubts the book skates past. Reply rates mix the sender’s judgement with the recipient’s taste. Women rarely write first, so the women who do may not represent women. Height, weight, income and education differ across groups, so a gap that looks like race can be a gap in those. Speed-dating studies, that note says, find weaker effects than OkCupid’s rhetoric. None of this proves the charts are empty. It proves they are not a verdict you can lift off the page and apply to “who we are.”

The book is still readable, and that is part of the problem. Rudder is funny, and the charts are built to be screenshot. A funny chart about other people’s racism is an easy way to feel like the adult in the room. The adult move would have been to show the design, the non-American counterexample, and the difference between a rating and a slur, at the speed of the joke rather than after it.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, what people click and what they say they believe can diverge, and dating is a place the divergence is measurable. Second, a photograph now travels with applications, messages and jobs, so a bias that used to wait until the interview can fire at the thumbnail. Third, “most attractive” on a website is not “most attractive once you are in the room,” which is a kindness hidden inside a bleak chart. Fourth, a dataset collected for one purpose becomes a different moral object when it is published as a mirror of society. Fifth, race statistics without a description of the sample and the question are not bravery. They are a dare.

Read it if you want to see how a platform’s founder talks about the people on it, and if you will keep O’Neil or Few beside you while you do. It is a poor book to cite as proof that “the data say” any particular group is unwanted. It is a useful book if you design products that rank human beings, because it shows how easily the ranking becomes a thesis, and how little the thesis asks permission. The line to argue with is the one about the accident. There was no accident. There was a company, a log, and a decision to treat the log as a confession.
