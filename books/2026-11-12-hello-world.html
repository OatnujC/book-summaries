Hannah Fry · 2018 · summarised 12 November 2026

Hello World: Hannah Fry on when to hand the decision to a machine

Hannah Fry’s Hello World, published in Britain in 2018 as How to Be Human in the Age of the Machine and in the United States under the related subtitle Being Human in the Age of Algorithms, is a mathematician’s tour of the software already sitting inside medicine, courts, cars, policing and art. Fry, a professor who is also a public communicator, is not writing another alarm in the vein of Cathy O’Neill’s Weapons of Math Destruction. A computer scientist reviewing the book for the Geomblog said as much with relief. After years of reading about algorithms as tools of bias, it was startling to meet a book that still likes computation, explains how models are trained, and then asks, case by case, whether the human should let go. The question has only got louder since 2018. Fry’s answer is not “never” and not “always”. It is a set of distinctions that many later arguments still blur.

The core argument

On Science Friday she put the distinction in plain speech. There are situations in which you want the best prediction you can get, and if an algorithm can show it predicts better than a person, you should hand over some control. Screening biopsy slides for cancer is her example. The systems have problems, and they have to be designed around those problems. But if a model is more sensitive than a pathologist who looks at hundreds of slides a day, and it can catch a tiny sign a tired eye misses, then trusting the model over the human alone is the humane choice. Criminal justice is the other pile. There, she said, “we have to be really, really careful and think very long and hard about how much control we hand over and the ways that we do that.” Accuracy is not the only virtue when the prediction changes someone’s liberty, and when the data are a record of past policing rather than a clean measure of future harm.

The book is organised around power and data, then around domains: justice, medicine, cars, crime, art. It starts with how algorithms work and how machine learning fits a model to examples, then moves quickly to cases, many of them British, which the Geomblog reviewer was glad to see. American readers can treat Silicon Valley as the whole map. Fry’s map includes the systems already scoring people in the UK. She is willing to talk about false positives and false negatives, and about Bayes, without pretending the reader has come for a textbook. A British Journal of General Practice reviewer who expected to hate a mathematics book called it an “algorithmic page turner”, which is the tone she is aiming at: serious enough to be used, light enough to be finished.

The cases she wants you to sit with

Justice is the chapter that has drawn the most technical argument. Predictive tools such as COMPAS, used in American courts to estimate the risk that someone will be arrested again, look like neutral scores and behave like arguments about fairness. Norman Fenton, a probabilist, wrote that Fry is right about the trade-off. You cannot maximise “accuracy” and a chosen definition of fairness at the same time when the groups in the data have different base rates. He also thinks the book, by leaving out the explicit false-positive arithmetic, can leave a careful reader unconvinced. You need Bayes, and some assumptions, to see why men and women, or any two groups, can be treated “equally” by one metric and very unequally by another. His complaint is friendly. The conclusion stands. The worked example is what a popular book sacrifices, and what a policy argument cannot.

Medicine is where Fry is warmer, and where the Geomblog reviewer wanted her colder. Biology is complex enough that a model might see a pattern a clinic has missed. That hope is real, and so is the record of tools trained on the wrong bodies, of false alarms that send healthy people into a cascade of tests, and of systems that work in the paper and fail on a Tuesday in a different hospital. Fry does not ignore the failures. The reviewer still felt the chapter let the profession off more lightly than the justice chapter, which is explicit that some decisions should not be automated just because a number is available. Cars and navigation supply the parable of trust. A Guardian review describes her linking a man who nearly followed a satnav off a cliff to Garry Kasparov at the chessboard and to voters nudged by social media. The mechanism is the same. A system that is right often enough becomes a habit, and the habit does not announce the day it is wrong. Doctors miss cancers. Algorithms miss them too, differently. The skill Fry wants is not loyalty to one side.

Art and power sit at the edges so the book cannot be read as a manual for cops and clinics only. If a machine can produce a passable image, or flag a face in a crowd, the question is who chose the objective and who lives with the error. Fry keeps returning to partnership: use the machine where it sees what we miss, and keep a person where the cost of a mistake is a life, a sentence or a public lie. The Geomblog reviewer, who works on fairness, recognised the questions the field was already asking. Are there better ways to combine human and automated judgement? What happens when the system starts to change the world it measures? Who decides what error rate is tolerable, and for whom? Fry alludes to those questions. A specialist wishes she had pressed them harder, and notes that some of them already had research answers in 2018 that the book does not stop to use.

What critics and readers push back on

The split between justice and medicine is the criticism that sticks. Caution in one chapter and optimism in the next can look like a mood rather than a principle, unless the principle is stated as sharply as she stated it on the radio: hand over control when the prediction is the whole point and the model has earned it, and refuse when the decision is a judgement about a person that a dataset of past judgements will only echo. Fenton’s note is the other durable objection. Popular clarity sometimes hides the step that makes the paradox true. Readers who want a prosecuted case against the industry will find Fry too fond of the machinery. Readers who want a celebration will find her too ready to talk about people who were scored, stopped or missed. That double dissatisfaction is a sign she is in the right argument. It is also a sign the book will not settle it. Published before the latest wave of generative tools, it cannot speak to systems that write and draw. It can still speak to the older, more entrenched systems that decide a biopsy, a benefit or a bail.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, “the algorithm is biased” and “the algorithm is more accurate” can both be true, and the argument is about which error you will accept. Second, a better prediction is a reason to give up control only in tasks where prediction is the job. A cancer slide is closer to that than a sentence. Third, fairness is not one number. Change the definition and the same tool looks just or unjust. Fourth, trust is a habit, and habits follow satnavs towards cliffs. Fifth, the interesting design is a division of labour, not a winner.

Read it if you want a clear, British-centred account of algorithms in public services without either a sales pitch or a sermon. It is especially good for clinicians, lawyers, journalists and anyone who has to explain a score to a person affected by it. It is less useful if you already work on fairness research and you want the proofs, or if you want a book about chatbots. Fry’s radio sentence is still the one to keep. If the model can see the tumour the pathologist misses, trust it, carefully. If the model is about to help decide who is dangerous, think very long and hard, and do not let the confidence of the number do the thinking for you.
