Min Jin Lee · 2017 · summarised 12 November 2026

Pachinko: Min Jin Lee’s family saga of Koreans who cannot go home

Min Jin Lee’s Pachinko, published in 2017, follows four generations of one Korean family from a fishing village under Japanese occupation to the late years of the twentieth century in Japan. The New York Times called it a family saga in which history itself is a character. That is the right scale. The novel is not a puzzle and not a romance, though it contains a seduction that never stops echoing. It is a book about people who are told, in one country and then another, that they do not quite belong, and about the work, the bargains and the silences they use to stay alive anyway. Pachinko, the pinball-like gambling game that fills Japanese parlours, is both a job and a metaphor: a game of chance that looks like skill, played by people who are shut out of the occupations that would let them be ordinary.

The family, and what happens to it

The story begins in Yeongdo, at the southern tip of Korea, around the time Japan annexes the country in 1910. An ageing couple take in lodgers. Their son Hoonie, born with a cleft palate and a twisted foot, makes a loving marriage to Yangjin and they run a boarding house. He dies of tuberculosis when their daughter Sunja is thirteen. A few years later Sunja is seduced by Koh Hansu, a wealthy fish broker with a wife and children in Japan and ties to the yakuza. He will support her. He will not marry her. She refuses to be his mistress. Into the boarding house comes Baek Isak, a sickly young Presbyterian minister on his way to Osaka. Sunja and her mother nurse him. When he learns she is pregnant, he offers his name. Sunja accepts. They leave for Japan.

In Osaka they live with Isak’s brother Yoseb and his wife Kyunghee, in Ikaino, a poor Korean neighbourhood. Sunja’s first son, Noa, is Hansu’s child and does not know it. Her second, Mozasu, is Isak’s. The war tightens around them. Christians are made to worship the emperor. When a church sexton prays the Lord’s Prayer instead, Isak is arrested with him. Sunja and Kyunghee sell food from a cart, then cook for a restaurant whose manager, it later turns out, works for Hansu. Isak comes home after years in prison, starved and broken, and dies. Hansu reappears, warns that the city will be bombed, and moves the family to a farm. He finds Yangjin. He finds Yoseb after the Nagasaki bombing. Protection and possession are the same gesture in his hands.

After the war the sons split. Noa studies his way towards Waseda University, determined to be the good Korean who cannot be mocked. Mozasu, less bookish, takes a job in a pachinko parlour and rises. When Sunja asks Hansu for a loan for Noa’s fees, she learns he has already paid them. Noa’s girlfriend notices the resemblance. Noa confronts his mother, decides that “yakuza blood” is “something that controls you,” leaves university, and spends sixteen years in Nagano passing as a Japanese office man, with a wife and four children, running the books of another parlour. In 1978 Sunja and Hansu find him. He kills himself. Mozasu marries Yumi, a Korean seamstress. They have a son, Solomon. A drunk driver kills Yumi. Sunja gives up her sweet shop to raise the boy. Mozasu wants Solomon in Western schools and an American firm, anywhere but the stained business that made the money. Solomon goes to Columbia, returns to a British bank in Tokyo, and is fired after an old Korean woman blocking a property deal is reached through Goro, the parlour boss. His Korean-American girlfriend leaves when he will not go back to America. A dying Hana, the daughter of Mozasu’s Japanese partner Etsuko, tells him to stop pretending. He goes to work for his father. The novel ends at Isak’s grave. The groundskeeper tells Sunja that Noa had kept visiting, all those years he would not see her.

What the novel is about

Belonging is the argument, and it is never settled by paperwork. Koreans in Japan, Zainichi, remain marked even when they are born there, speak Japanese, and take Japanese names at school. The novel keeps showing the small machinery of that mark: the job that is not offered, the insult in a playground, the bank that likes Solomon until it sees who his father’s friends are. Pachinko is where excluded people could make money, and also the business that lets everyone else call them criminals. Mozasu is honest inside a trade the country has decided is dirty. Solomon’s last choice is to trust that honesty rather than keep auditioning for a respectability that can be withdrawn.

The women carry the other half of the book. A line the New York Times later used as a discussion question is put to Sunja early: “a woman’s life is endless work and suffering … For a woman, the man you marry will determine the quality of your life completely.” Sunja’s life tests the proverb without quite obeying it. Isak gives her a name and a passage, then dies. Hansu offers money and takes decisions she did not ask for. Yangjin, Kyunghee and Sunja keep the household by cooking, selling and refusing to collapse in public. Their agency is not a speech. It is the cart, the shop, the decision to tell or not tell a son who his father is. Lee, who lived in Tokyo while researching the novel, writes them in a plain style that trusts accumulation. Reviewers have compared the span to Dickens and to Galsworthy: many rooms, many years, and a social order that grinds whether or not any one person is cruel on a given day.

Blood is the cruellest idea in the book, and Lee does not endorse it. Noa spends his youth trying to be impeccable and then decides that Hansu’s world is in his veins. The suicide is not a twist for its own sake. It is what happens when a person accepts the story his persecutors tell about him. The graveside visit is the novel’s answer, and it is quiet. He had not stopped being Sunja’s son. He had only stopped being able to live as one in the open.

Why it is loved, and what it leaves out

Readers love the scale and the steadiness. History arrives as annexation, war, the bomb, the post-war boom and the bubble, but it arrives in kitchens. The Times headline caught that: history is a character, not a lecture. The Apple TV adaptation later widened the audience, which is a mixed gift. The show is lush where the novel is plain, and the plainness is part of the point. Lee’s control is that she does not pause to explain Koreans in Japan to a foreign reader at length. She lets the exclusions happen again and again until the pattern is the explanation.

The complaints are real. Four generations and a large cast mean some later figures, Solomon’s colleagues among them, are thinner than Sunja. Hansu can feel like a plot device, always rich enough to rescue or ruin the family at the moment the chapter needs it. A few readers find the prose deliberately flat, closer to chronicle than to the sentence-making of a more lyrical novel. And anyone who wants the politics of Zainichi life as argument rather than as lived weather will find Lee withholding. She is more interested in how a woman sells sweets, and how a boy decides his blood is cursed, than in a chapter of history. That is a choice, not an omission she forgot to fix.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, survival in this novel is not a single brave act. It is cooking, studying, lying by omission, and going to work in a business you did not want. Second, a secret kept to protect a child can become the thing that destroys him, and a secret told too late can do the same. Third, money from the excluded trade does not buy the respect the straight world advertises. Solomon learns that in one firing. Fourth, the people who love you may still hand you a story about taint. Noa dies of that story, not of poverty. Fifth, the last scene matters more than the suicide: he kept going to the grave of the man who gave him a name.

Read it if you want a long novel about migration that is not a moral lesson with costumes, and if you can bear a plot that includes imprisonment, war and a suicide. It is a poor choice if you want experimental form or a tight mystery. It is a very good choice if you have ever been told that working hard and speaking the language would be enough, and you would like to watch a family test that promise over eighty years. Lee’s answer is not that the promise was a total lie. It is that the people who kept it were still asked to be grateful for a game they were never meant to win.
