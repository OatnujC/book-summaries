Hans Rosling, with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund · 2018 · summarised 12 November 2026

Factfulness: Hans Rosling’s case that the world is bad, and much less bad than you think

Factfulness, published in 2018, is Hans Rosling’s last book, written with his son Ola Rosling and his daughter-in-law Anna Rosling Rönnlund, and finished as a posthumous fight against what he called devastating ignorance. Kirkus describes the method. Ten “instincts” push people toward an overdramatic picture: splitting the world in two, blaming one villain for many troubles, assuming the trend is downward. Charts, jokes and a run of multiple-choice questions are meant to catch the reader being wrong, and then to show the trend that was available all along. Rosling does not say the world is fine. Kirkus notes the five risks he still wants watched: poverty, global warming, financial collapse, a pandemic, and a third world war. The book’s claim is narrower and more irritating. On a long list of human welfare measures, the public, including the educated public, systematically picks the gloomy option, and the gloomy option is out of date.

The core argument

The evidence he leans on is a Gapminder survey of simple questions about health and population. A Current Affairs essay reports the result he made famous. The average person scored about two out of twelve. More education did not help. “Some of the most appalling results came from a group of Nobel laureates and medical researchers.” He liked to say a chimpanzee would beat the humans, meaning people did worse than chance, and that the errors leaned negative. The worldview he wants instead is the one the same essay quotes. Most of the world’s population is in the middle of the income scale. “Perhaps they are not what we think of as middle class, but they are not living in extreme poverty. Their girls go to school, their children get vaccinated, they live in two-child families, and they want to go abroad on holiday, not as refugees.” Step by step, “the world is improving. Not on every single measure every single year, but as a rule.” That last sentence is the whole book. Factfulness is not a list of statistics to memorise. It is a practice of noticing when an instinct has chosen the story before the number.

The instincts are his names for those shortcuts. The gap instinct wants two clumps, rich and poor, developed and developing, with a canyon between them. The negativity instinct treats a bad headline as a trend. The fear instinct lingers on what is dramatic. The blame instinct looks for a villain, “evil businessmen” among them, when a system of interacting causes would be truer. The destiny instinct decides that a country, a religion or a culture is the way it is for good. The single-perspective instinct assumes one lever will fix a mess; American health care, in his telling, is a case of too much faith in markets and not enough of a change in mindset. Kirkus adds the urge to generalise and the urge to feel urgent. A numeracy review lists the same family and notes that the illustrations come from classrooms, lecture halls and years of front-line medicine, not only from a spreadsheet.

What the charts are for

The charts are a correction to a mental picture formed in the 1960s and never updated: a rich West, a poor remainder, crisis as the normal weather. Rosling’s point, which even a hostile essay grants before it quarrels, is that extreme poverty, child mortality and deaths from many disasters have fallen, and that a person who does not know this will misread every new crisis as proof that nothing works. He also, Current Affairs notes, talks about “5 billion potential consumers” in Asia and Africa, and about people who have “escaped misery and become consumers and producers for the world market.” The moral of the chart, in his hands, is not only that suffering has shrunk. It is that the people who left the worst poverty are now part of the same economy as the reader, with wants that look ordinary. Vaccination is the cleaner example. The essay cites his own style of claim in the polio figures: most of the world’s infants protected, and a large remainder still not. He wants both halves of that sentence. Admirers remember the first. Critics think he rushes the second.

The media chapter, as a critical review by Christian Berggren retells it, uses a proportion that is hard to forget. In two weeks of 2009, 31 swine-flu deaths were covered by 253,442 articles, more than 8,000 per death, while about 63,000 tuberculosis deaths in the same period drew a tenth of an article each. You do not need Rosling’s evolutionary stories to see what that ratio does to a person’s sense of risk. A new disease with a camera crew will feel larger than an old disease without one. Factfulness asks you to divide before you feel. That request is the part of the book that survives the argument about his politics.

What critics and readers push back on

Current Affairs thinks the instincts are not facts in the sense the title promises. Rosling, the essay says, offers anecdotes and just-so stories about evolution, and points in the notes to popular psychology, including Steven Pinker, without a serious discussion of whether the instincts have been shown to exist. The gap between rich-country guesses and the data is real. The theory that ten named instincts explain the gap is a teacher’s device. The same essay dislikes the politics that the device smuggles in. “Escaped misery,” it argues, is a strange phrase for a family on a few dollars a day, one illness away from the line they just crossed, a fragility Rosling himself describes. Blame directed at a system rather than at a company can become a reason never to name a company. A mindset problem is an easier diagnosis than a lobby. Berggren’s review, written from a worry about population and migration, thinks the book’s sunny brief underplays pressures Rosling the television guest had already waved aside. The two critiques do not agree with each other. They agree that a chart of progress is not a policy, and that Rosling often talks as if the chart were the hard part.

Kirkus is content to call the result a sunny prognosis and a sigh of relief. That is fair to the tone and unfair to the five risks, which are sitting in the book waiting for a reader who only wanted permission to relax. The risk of the genre, shared with other “new optimist” arguments, is that a person leaves immune to bad news rather than better at weighing it. Rosling’s own rule, not on every measure every year, is the antidote, and it is easier to quote than to use when the measure in front of you is the one going the wrong way.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, if experts and laureates miss a basic question in the same direction, the error is in the picture, not in a lack of intelligence. Update the picture. Second, a divided world of “developed” and “developing” hides the large middle where most people now live. Third, dramatic deaths crowd out ordinary deaths. Divide the coverage by the bodies before you rank the fears. Fourth, progress on child survival does not cancel climate risk, pandemic risk or the chance of war. He listed them. Do not drop them because the tone was cheerful. Fifth, an instinct is a label. It is not yet an explanation, and it is a poor substitute for a political argument about who should do what.

Read it if your sense of the world was formed by disaster footage and a school textbook that stopped decades ago. It is a bad book to use as a reason not to act, and a good book to use before you act on a false trend. The sentence worth keeping is the one about the rule and the exceptions. The world can be improving as a rule while the exception in front of you is the one that ruins lives. Factfulness is the habit of knowing which is which. It is not the habit of feeling better and stopping there.
