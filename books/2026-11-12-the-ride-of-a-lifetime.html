Robert Iger · 2019 · summarised 12 November 2026

The Ride of a Lifetime: Robert Iger’s deals, and the calm he will not take off

Robert Iger’s The Ride of a Lifetime, published by Random House in 2019, is a chief executive’s book about fifteen years at the top of Disney, and about the longer climb that got him there. The American subtitle promises lessons from those years. A British edition’s subtitle, noted by a reviewer at IMD, shifts the accent to creative leadership. The difference matters, because the creativity in the book mostly belongs to other people. Bloomberg says the inspirational opening gives way to something better: a career in a business full of large egos, from a man who spent almost a decade in the shadow of Michael Eisner and took the company in 2005, then bought Pixar, Marvel, Lucasfilm and, later, a large piece of 21st Century Fox. The deals are the reason to read it. The restraint is the reason it frustrates. Iger has written a book as disciplined as the person who, several reviews note, gets up at 4.15 in the morning to exercise. You learn how he won. You learn less about what it cost him to keep winning politely.

The core argument

The lesson, repeated until it becomes the structure, is that a creative company grows by acquiring other creative companies and then leaving them alone enough to stay themselves. IMD’s review watches the pattern chapter by chapter. An audacious goal, a personal courtship of a reluctant seller, a promise that the acquired culture will be protected, a result that is said to have left everyone better off. Pixar in 2006, Marvel in 2009, Lucasfilm in 2012, Fox after that. There is also a deal that was not made, for Twitter, which at least shows the reflex could be refused. Variety finds the book liveliest in those passages, and in the small human details around them. Half an hour before Disney and Pixar were to announce the sale, Steve Jobs walked Iger outside and said the cancer had come back, and that Iger could still walk away from a deal that would make Jobs Disney’s largest individual shareholder. Iger did not walk away. “Instead of toasting what had been a momentous day in my early days as CEO, we cried together over the news.” The Hollywood Reporter adds the earlier rupture: Eisner’s fight with Jobs had left the relationship looking finished, and Iger’s move was to woo Jobs himself. Animation, the field that mattered most to Disney’s idea of itself, was bought back by refusing to inherit a feud.

Marvel is told as a problem of temperament. Peter Bart, in Deadline, reports Iger’s sense that Ike Perlmutter “did not respond well to Hollywood slickness,” and the decision to pull Kevin Feige’s film unit out from under the rest of the company. The same review says Iger had to push past resistance inside Marvel to a woman or a Black lead, and that Black Panther sat on the far side of that push. Fox, in the Hollywood Reporter’s telling, turns on a glass of wine at Rupert Murdoch’s vineyard. These are good scenes. They support a theory of leadership as courtship plus nerve: find the person who can actually say yes, offer respect rather than a conquest, and do not blink at the last moment. They support it because the deals worked. A method demonstrated only by its successes is a memoir’s natural bias. Iger does not pretend to be a neutral historian of the firm.

What he will not dwell on

The Hollywood Reporter is blunt about the wall. If you want the deals, the book is often thrilling, and each one was a leap that hindsight has made to look inevitable. If you want the man, you meet a diplomat. Michael Ovitz’s arrival as president in 1995, a notorious mess in the industry’s memory, is reduced to “a moment of disappointment,” plus the news that Iger now has a cordial relationship with him, “perhaps the only person in the entire studio who does.” Eisner is praised as a mentor even after, no longer chief executive, he took his doubts about the Pixar deal to the board. John Lasseter’s exit after harassment allegations gets a few sentences: Iger had “spoken to John about it some years back,” then he and Alan Horn agreed a six-month leave in November 2017, and later “a complete exit from Disney was wise.” The review hears the missing question. Spoken to, some years back, and then what? A book that will not sit in that silence is a book choosing its lessons. Composure is one of them. Accountability for a culture is a lesson it states more than it shows.

IMD makes a different cut, and it is the one a strategy reader should keep. The creativity he describes is Roone Arledge’s in sports television, David Lynch’s in Twin Peaks, Eisner’s eye for a park, John Lasseter and Ed Catmull’s storytelling at Pixar. Iger’s gift, on this account, is to recognise those gifts, pay for them, and stop other people from interfering. That is real work. It is not the same work as making the film. The British subtitle flatters it into creative leadership. The chapters are the art of the acquisition. Whenever the company faced a choice to build or to buy, the review notes, it bought, including the technology behind its streaming plans. Acquisitions often fail in the wider record. These did not, or had not by 2019. The book is thin on how the balance sheet allowed the spree, which is the sort of omission a chief executive can afford and a student of the firm cannot.

What critics and readers push back on

Bloomberg thinks the public already knew the outline and did not know the manoeuvres, and that the manoeuvres are worth the price. The Hollywood Reporter agrees about the deals and thinks the diplomacy becomes the limitation: more scenes like the Jobs walk and the missing Perlmutter, fewer sentences that could have been said of anyone. Bart is the most personally generous. He has found Iger gracious in life, and he reads the book as a patient man outlasting louder rivals, including the choice to present himself as an underdog rather than as a corporate warrior. The three reviews can all be right. A gracious operator can also write a gracious book that sands the edges off the operations. The sanding is not a lie. It is a selection, and the selection is the character.

There is a further limit of timing. The book ends while the acquired stories are still a triumph and the streaming bet is young. A reader now knows more about how those bets felt later. That later knowledge is not a fact in the memoir, and it should not be smuggled back in as if Iger had withheld it. What he withheld, the reviews are clear, was already withheld in 2019: the ugly middle of a personnel crisis, the money, the parts of Eisner that do not fit a mentor story.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, a damaged relationship with a key partner can be more dangerous than a gap in the product line. Iger treated the Jobs feud as the problem to solve, not as a condition to inherit. Second, buying a creative culture and then directing it like a division is a way to kill what you paid for. The promise of respect was the product. Third, nerve at the last half-hour, when you are offered a reason to delay, is a different skill from having the idea. Fourth, composure can be a way of not saying what happened. Read the Lasseter pages for the gaps. Fifth, recognising other people’s talent is a job. Calling it creativity of your own is how executive memoirs usually flatter the buyer.

Read it for the deals, especially Pixar and Marvel, if you care how a large company talks a founder into a sale. It is a poor book if you want a confession, a financial case, or a theory of how stories are made. Iger is clear, brisk and, as IMD says, upbeat, a man writing near the end of a long run and still in control of the tone. The scene to remember is the walk with Jobs, thirty minutes before the announcement, and the decision not to flinch. The scene the book will not give you is the one in which the flinch would have been the honest thing. That absence is part of the lesson, whether or not it is the lesson he intended.
