Patrick McGee · 2025 · summarised 12 November 2026

Apple in China: Patrick McGee’s account of how a supply chain became a trap

On 13 May 2025, Scribner published Patrick McGee’s Apple in China: The Capture of the World’s Greatest Company. McGee is a Financial Times journalist who has covered Apple for years, and the book is his attempt to retell a story most people think they already know. The usual version is that Apple designs beautiful objects in California and has them assembled cheaply in China. McGee thinks that version is backwards, or at least badly incomplete. “It would be banal to say that Apple wouldn’t be Apple today without China,” he writes. “What this book contends is more intriguing—that China wouldn’t be China today without Apple.” Bob Davis, reviewing the book in Foreign Policy, calls the line hyperbolic and then says McGee makes the case convincingly. The claim matters because the iPhone is no longer only a consumer product. It is a piece of industrial policy, sitting in the middle of the argument between Washington and Beijing about who gets to make the advanced world.

The core argument

Apple’s dependence on China, in this account, is not a story about low wages alone, though low wages were the bait. It is a story about knowledge. To get factories to meet its standards, Apple sent its own engineers to teach local partners how to mass-produce new technology at very low prices, and in doing so it gave the manufacturing knowledge away. Davis, borrowing McGee’s phrase, calls this “the Apple Squeeze”. Contractors accept thin margins, sometimes no profit at all, because the learning and the prestige let them win other customers who will pay more. Beijing encourages the arrangement because it understands the transfer. Over the years Apple has trained a large body of Chinese engineers and managers, and its contractors have employed millions of workers. McGee’s short description of the company, which Davis quotes, is “the world’s largest manufacturer without owning factories.”

The political consequence arrives later. Once the know-how, the specialised machines and the cluster of suppliers are in China, leaving is no longer a purchasing decision. It is an attempt to rebuild a civilisation of production somewhere else, while the state that hosts the old one has every lever, from regulation to shipping delays, to make the move hurt. Kirkus Reviews, which called the book “a well-argued, eye-opening look at the dark side of globalism”, puts the end state bluntly. Under Xi Jinping, Apple’s freedom of movement has been curtailed, and yet the savings keep it captured, even as it placates the government. The capture is technical as well as political. The company that once sold itself as the enemy of conformity has become, in McGee’s telling, a pillar of a rival industrial power.

How Apple got there

The early chapters are a reminder that China was not the plan. In the early 1990s Apple still wanted to show it could build computers in the United States and keep design and production under one roof. Davis quotes a Jobs biography on Macs rolling off one American line “like a Holiday Inn toaster turns out toasted bagels.” Facing bankruptcy in 1996, the company sold its factory in Colorado and started outsourcing in earnest, first at home and then in Asia. There had been experiments before that: Canon in Japan built the LaserWriter, Japanese firms made some laptops and the Newton, and Apple tried South Korea, Singapore, Ireland, Wales and Mexico. The relationship that mattered was with Foxconn in Taiwan, whose founder, Terry Gou, was as obsessive as Steve Jobs.

Taiwan could not supply the workers or the room. Gou, part of the generation of Taiwanese manufacturers who invested heavily on the mainland, moved production to China, where the workforce looked unlimited and local governments offered land, infrastructure and a political climate that suited a harsh shop floor. Davis is plain about what that climate included: a crackdown on independent worker organisations, and a willingness to overlook child labour and extreme overtime. Apple then pulled manufacturing that had been scattered across Asia into China. Turnover could run to several hundred per cent a year. Other Western firms arrived too, often in joint ventures that became a route for local partners to take technology. Apple tried not to fall into that trap. “Instead of selecting components off the shelf,” McGee writes, “Apple was designing custom parts, crafting the manufacturing behind them, and orchestrating their assembly into enormously complex systems.” The aim was to “build moats around its manufacturing innovations.” One moat was ownership: Apple bought the expensive machines and tried to make sure they were used only for Apple.

Gou, known inside Apple as Uncle Terry, was also the company’s early guide to Chinese politics. He knew how to collect the free land and subsidies that local officials handed out, and those officials had their own reason to help. Factories that hired large numbers of people, and that advanced Beijing’s wish to climb the electronics industry, were good for a career. For a long time this was enough. Apple could look like a customer. Foxconn could look like the company that dealt with the state.

That pretence got harder around 2013, as Xi Jinping consolidated power and Apple drew hostile coverage as a bullying Western firm. The company assembled a “Gang of Eight”, managers with China experience, and they had to learn a distinction Davis draws from the reporting: the Western idea of the rule of law, and rule by law, in which the Party sets the rules. When Beijing wanted apps gone, Apple removed the New York Times and 674 VPN apps from the Chinese store, though not, Davis notes, from the global one. It invested in firms it believed the government favoured, including Didi Chuxing and the chipmaker YMTC, and sited a data centre in Guizhou on a political bet that did not pay off. Apple dropped the project in 2022.

The money, as McGee presents it to Chinese officials, is a claim of investment on the order of $55 billion a year, and in 2016 a plan to spend $275 billion over five years. Davis says McGee never quite shows how those figures were built. Kirkus reports the same order of spending by 2015, “possibly much more”, until Apple had become, in McGee’s words, “the world’s biggest corporate investor” in the country, while earning about $70 billion a year from China as a market. The flow runs both ways. China is the workshop and one of the best customers, and the customer has a government attached.

Apple also shifted work from Taiwanese-owned manufacturers towards Chinese-owned ones, the “red supply chain” of McGee’s phrase. Davis quotes the conclusion McGee draws from the technology transfer: it “made it the biggest corporate supporter of Made in China 2025”, Beijing’s plan to lead in the technologies that matter. How Apple stayed in favour in Washington is a question Davis thinks the book cannot answer. Cook met Donald Trump fairly often in the first term, but the conversations are not there. Apple’s products were among those excused from tariffs in that first trade war, and Washington restricted advanced chips to Huawei, Apple’s strongest Chinese rival. McGee, Davis says, leans towards Apple having been a lucky bystander. Davis finds that hard to believe of Cook.

The book was finished before the later tariff shock. Davis notes Apple’s later pledge to spend heavily in the United States, and Trump’s threat of a tariff of at least 25 per cent on iPhones made anywhere abroad. India is the diversification everyone mentions. McGee treats it as a long project, and an incomplete one: even if final assembly moves, much of what goes inside the phone still comes from China, and copying a system built around Apple-owned machines could cost hundreds of billions. China may not allow a quiet exit. Antitrust cases, regulatory trouble and delays at the port are available if Beijing decides leaving should hurt. Doug Guthrie, one of the Gang of Eight, wrote after he left: “Quite simply, you don’t get to do business in China today without doing what the Chinese government wants you to do. Period. No one is immune. No one.”

What critics and readers push back on

Kirkus is admiring. It calls the book an exposé of how the search for profit put a company that once prized its own hardware and software “into the orbit of a totalitarian regime”, and it thinks the likely ending is unhappy: Huawei taking market share, and trade wars damaging the business in the near term. The details it picks out are the ones that clash with the old advertising. A Chinese scholar’s description of the competitiveness Apple found so useful, “low wages, low welfare, and low human rights”, does not sit easily with “think different”. Nor does Apple blocking VPNs, or restricting AirDrop “after it emerged that Chinese citizens were using it to organize.”

Davis’s objection is about proportion. The book tells Apple’s story as a tale of brilliant, demanding men, Jobs, Cook and Gou, whose admitted faults are arrogance and neglect of their families. “This sometimes gets to be too much, like listening to a person who says his biggest flaw is that he works too hard.” The book is silent, he says, on how Apple and its peers, by shedding American factory jobs, fed the shock of Chinese imports, and nearly as quiet about the worst conditions at suppliers. McGee chides journalists for staring at the suicides at Foxconn and missing the larger story. He concedes that “Periodic exposes helped to shine a light on working conditions and likely caused some positive change,” and then argues that the press still missed the strategy. A reader can agree the strategic story had not been told, and still think the book owes the injured more than a rebuke to the reporters who noticed them. The Washington half is thin in the same way. A company this entangled did not stay in favour by accident, and a history that cannot say what Cook and Trump discussed has a hole in its politics.

The China half of the reporting is what justifies the book. The risk is that a reader comes away impressed by the ingenuity of the Squeeze and only afterwards notices who was squeezed.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

The lessons sit at the join between a company and a state. First, a supply chain is a school. Apple did not only buy labour in China. It taught a way of making things, and teaching is a transfer whether or not the contract says so. Second, the moat and the gift can be the same act. Custom parts and Apple-owned machines were meant to stop copying, and they also pulled a whole cluster of suppliers into existence around Foxconn’s hubs. Third, political risk does not arrive when the chief executive flies to Beijing. It arrives when the factories, the engineers and the revenue are already there, and the cost of leaving has become a second balance sheet. Fourth, accommodation is a sequence, not a single scandal: an app removed, a data centre sited to flatter a politician, a product feature restricted because people were using it to organise. Guthrie’s line is the sum of them.

Read the full book if you want the reporting, and if you have heard “China” and “Apple” in the same sentence for twenty years and still pictured a simple outsourcing deal. It is less useful as a labour history or a Washington history. Davis is right that the central claim is bold, and that McGee largely earns it, and also right that the book is kinder to its protagonists than the material is. The sentence to keep is McGee’s. China would not be China today without Apple. Whether Apple can still be Apple anywhere else may no longer be Apple’s question to answer alone.
