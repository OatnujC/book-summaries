Peter Thiel with Blake Masters · 2014 · summarised 12 November 2026

Zero to One: Peter Thiel’s case that competition is a trap

Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future, published by Crown Business in September 2014, is Peter Thiel’s startup book, written with Blake Masters from the notes Masters took in CS183, the class Thiel taught at Stanford in the spring of 2012. Thiel had co-founded PayPal and was an early investor in Facebook. The book’s title is a distinction he wants founders to feel in their stomachs. Copying a known business takes the world from one to n, more of something familiar. Inventing takes it from zero to one. The next Bill Gates will not build an operating system, and the next Larry Page will not build a search engine. The book still circulates because it flatters a particular ambition, the desire to be alone in a market, and because it says that ambition in the language of a contrarian who thinks the age of mobile phones has disguised a wider stagnation.

The core argument

Thiel thinks the culture has an unhealthy love of competition. He calls it, in Timothy B. Lee’s summary for Vox, an “ideology of competition”: the belief that rivalry is the path to success and prosperity. In a crowded trade, he argues, you are probably not doing anything original, and you will not make the profits that fund another original thing. Perfect competition competes profits away. A restaurant that has automated what it can, and worked everyone to the bone, earns just enough that the owner does not quit, because if the margin were fatter a rival would already have appeared. Scott Alexander, reviewing the book on Slate Star Codex, borrows Hobbes for that life: nasty, brutish and short. Monopoly is the escape. A company that can charge more than its costs, for a long time, because nobody can quite copy it, is the company that can think about the future rather than about this week’s price.

Lee’s objection is the one the book never quite absorbs. Nobody serious claims competition is pleasant for the firm inside it. The case for competition is that it is good for the customer. Thiel, Lee writes, “conflates these two claims, treating the fact that monopolies are good for monopolists as evidence that monopolies are good for society generally.” The book’s answer is that the monopolies worth having are the creative ones, built on something new, not the ones that corner a commodity. Whether that answer holds is the argument the rest of the chapters are trying to win.

How he says you build one

Alexander extracts four routes to a monopoly that lasts. Proprietary technology has to be decisively better, not a little better. Thiel’s provocative threshold, as Alexander reports it, is ten times. Twice as good and you are still in a fight. Nobody remembers whether Lycos or AltaVista was slightly ahead. Everyone remembers that Google was in another class, and that the fight then ended. Network effects are the Facebook story. A rival with more features still loses if your friends are elsewhere. The awkwardness is that a new firm has no network on day one. Thiel’s reply is to monopolise a tiny pool and expand. Facebook began with Harvard, then colleges, then everyone. PayPal began with eBay’s power sellers, became indispensable there, and spread. Amazon began as a bookshop. Economies of scale favour software in particular, because the next copy costs almost nothing, so the firm with more customers can charge less or keep more. Brand is the fourth, and the least respectable if it is only a logo. Apple can charge more than a no-name phone partly because non-experts use the brand as a certificate, and partly because people pay to be seen with the object. A brand that is only advertising, without a product underneath, does not survive the list.

Durability matters as much as the head start. A company’s value is the cash it will throw off later, so a fashion that burns bright and dies, a nightclub, a game studio that cannot follow one hit, a voucher site that cannot keep merchants, can be wildly overvalued. Thiel’s phrase for the firm that stays is the last mover, not the first. First-mover advantage is fragile. Someone improves on you. You become the last mover by sequencing markets: dominate a small, real one, then step into the next only when you are strong enough to keep it. “One per cent of a giant market” is, in this telling, a warning sign. That 1 per cent is hard, and the giants will grind the margin to nothing. Do not describe yourself as disruptive if you can avoid it. The word trains you to pick a fight. PayPal, on Thiel’s account, did not so much attack Visa as sit beside the cards and send them more volume than it took. There is also a chapter on secrets: the valuable fact other people are not seeing, which is the only honest reason a new company should exist. And there is a warning about founding teams, distribution, and the mistake of building a product nobody has to sell because “the product will sell itself.”

The stagnation claim frames all of this. Information technology has raced ahead. Thiel does not think that excuses the rest of the economy. Going from zero to one is allowed in energy, transport, medicine, anywhere a secret is still sitting. The definite optimist, in his typology, plans a future and builds it. The indefinite optimist, which he thinks describes a lot of modern finance and politics, expects the future to be better and refuses to say how. Venture capital, on this view, is not a diversified cloud of apps. It is a search for the rare company that escapes competition entirely.

What critics and readers push back on

Lee’s conflation point is the structural objection, and it has not aged out. A founder reading Thiel can come away thinking that high margins are a public service. Sometimes a new product really does leave customers better off and the firm rich. Sometimes the moat is lock-in, a tax on switching, or a regulator who never arrives. The book is more eloquent about the first case. Alexander is sympathetic to the monopoly chapters and still useful as a translator, because he states the restaurant logic without Thiel’s swagger. Other readers, including a later technical review, wanted the failures: monopolies that rot, industries where competition produced the progress, the long stretch in which yesterday’s unassailable platform becomes someone’s complement. The “ten times better” rule is a slogan that sorts anecdotes. It does not tell you, in advance, whether you are Google or a slightly quicker Lycos. And the political Thiel, which this book mostly keeps offstage, makes some readers hear the monopoly praise as a theory of power, not only of product. Masters’s notes turned a course into a creed. The creed travels well among people who already intend to start something, and it travels badly as a description of how most useful work gets done.

What’s worth keeping, and who should read it

First, copying is a different activity from inventing, and the second is rarer than pitch decks suggest. Second, a business that can be matched on price by anyone who tries will not fund a long future, however hard the team works. Third, start in a market small enough to win, and do not call a fantasy market a niche. Fourth, a head start is not a moat. Technology, a network, scale or a brand that is actually in the product might be. Fifth, do not confuse what is good for the owner of the moat with what is good for the person locked inside it. Lee’s distinction is the correction the book needs on the facing page.

Read it if you are starting something and you have been told that the way to win is to out-hustle a crowded field. It is short, and the examples from PayPal, Facebook and Google are the part worth arguing with rather than memorising. It is a bad sole guide to economics, to regulation, or to any career that is valuable precisely because it is not a monopoly. Thiel is at his best when he asks what you know that is both true and not yet believed. He is at his worst when the answer is allowed to be “a position nobody can take away from me,” and nobody asks who pays for that.
